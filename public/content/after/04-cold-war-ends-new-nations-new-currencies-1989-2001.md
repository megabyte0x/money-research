# 04 — The Cold War Ends: New Nations, New Currencies and the Euro, 1989–2001

## German reunification and the price of a 1:1 conversion

The Berlin Wall opened on 9 November 1989. On 1 July 1990, three months before political reunification, West Germany extended the Deutsche Mark to East Germany, converting wages, prices and small savings at one Ostmark to one Deutsche Mark, against the advice of the Bundesbank, which thought the East German mark was worth a quarter of that. The conversion made East German industry uncompetitive overnight and required transfers from West to East of roughly DM 100–150 billion a year through the 1990s, financed by borrowing. To contain the resulting inflation the Bundesbank raised its rates to 8.75% in 1992 — the highest since 1931 — at a moment when the rest of Europe, tied to the mark through the Exchange Rate Mechanism, was in recession and needed lower rates. That conflict caused the ERM crisis below.

## The Soviet collapse: fifteen new states, fifteen new currencies

The Soviet Union dissolved on 26 December 1991 into fifteen republics. For about two years most kept using the Soviet ruble, which each republic's central bank could create credit in — a "ruble zone" with fifteen printers and no coordination, which is a recipe for the fastest possible inflation. Russia liberalised prices on 2 January 1992 ("shock therapy," designed with Jeffrey Sachs and Yegor Gaidar); prices rose about 2,500% in 1992, wiping out Soviet-era savings. Russia expelled the other republics from the ruble zone in July 1993 by cancelling old notes.

The new currencies, with dates, tell the story of how a state establishes money from nothing:

Estonia's kroon (June 1992) was the model: a currency board fixed at 8 kroons to the Deutsche Mark, backed 100% by foreign reserves, which gave Estonia the region's lowest inflation and set it on the path to the euro (2011). Latvia's lats (1993) and Lithuania's litas (1993, pegged to the dollar from 1994) followed. Ukraine issued the karbovanets coupon (1992), which hyperinflated at over 10,000% in 1993, and replaced it with the hryvnia in September 1996. Kazakhstan's tenge (November 1993), Kyrgyzstan's som (May 1993), Uzbekistan's som, Belarus's ruble, Moldova's leu, Georgia's lari (1995), Armenia's dram (1993), Azerbaijan's manat (1992), Turkmenistan's manat (1993) and Tajikistan's ruble (1995, later somoni) completed the set. Every one of them lost most of its value in its first two years; every one eventually stabilised by adopting some version of the Western model — a central bank with a legal mandate for price stability, a peg or an inflation target, and IMF support.

Russia itself defaulted on its domestic debt and devalued the ruble by two-thirds in August 1998 (file 05), redenominated the currency 1,000:1 in 1998, and did not achieve stable money until the oil boom of the 2000s. The "loans-for-shares" privatisations of 1995–96 created the oligarchs and the resentment that Vladimir Putin, in power from 2000, built his rule on.

## Central Europe, Yugoslavia and the worst hyperinflation of the era

Poland's Balcerowicz Plan (1 January 1990) — price liberalisation, a fixed exchange rate, tight money, convertibility — was the first post-communist stabilisation and, after a brutal 1990–91, the most successful; Poland became the only EU economy to avoid recession in 2009. Czechoslovakia split peacefully into the Czech Republic and Slovakia on 1 January 1993, and their common currency lasted just five more weeks before being split on 8 February 1993 — an unusually clean example of a currency divorce. Hungary, Romania and Bulgaria took longer; Bulgaria hyperinflated in 1996–97 and stabilised with a currency board in July 1997.

Yugoslavia broke up in war from 1991 (file 06). Slovenia (tolar, October 1991) and Croatia (dinar 1991, kuna 1994) left first; Bosnia's war (1992–95) ended with the Dayton Accords and a currency board (the convertible mark, 1998) administered under international supervision. Rump Yugoslavia (Serbia and Montenegro), under UN sanctions and financing the war by printing, suffered the second-worst hyperinflation ever recorded: in January 1994 prices rose 313 million per cent in a single month, doubling every 34 hours; the government issued a 500-billion-dinar note. Montenegro adopted the Deutsche Mark unilaterally in 1999 and the euro in 2002 without being an EU member; Kosovo did the same after independence in 2008. Both are examples of "euroisation," the fiat-era equivalent of a small state using another's coin.

## Other independences and new currencies

The post-1971 era saw about fifty new states, and each faced the choice of issuing its own currency, joining a monetary union or adopting someone else's. Bangladesh (1971) issued the taka in 1972. Portugal's revolution freed Angola, Mozambique, Guinea-Bissau and Cape Verde in 1974–75; Mozambique's metical and Angola's kwanza both hyperinflated during their civil wars. Zimbabwe (1980) issued a dollar that by 2008 had reached an inflation rate estimated at 80 billion per cent a month, the worst since Hungary in 1946; the country abolished its currency in 2009, used the US dollar and rand, tried a new dollar in 2019, and in April 2024 launched the ZiG, backed by gold and foreign reserves — the only new gold-backed currency of the fiat era. Namibia (1990) issued a dollar pegged to the rand within the Common Monetary Area. Eritrea (1993) used the Ethiopian birr until issuing the nakfa in 1997, which contributed to the 1998–2000 border war. East Timor (2002) adopted the US dollar outright. South Sudan (July 2011) issued a pound that lost over 95% of its value in its civil war. Ecuador (2000) and El Salvador (2001) abandoned their own currencies for the US dollar after crises; El Salvador also made Bitcoin legal tender in 2021 and repealed that status in January 2025 under IMF pressure. The fourteen African countries of the CFA franc zone kept a currency pegged to the French franc (and from 1999 the euro) guaranteed by the French Treasury; its 50% devaluation in January 1994 was the largest single policy shock in post-colonial Africa, and the West African members announced in 2019 a reform toward an "eco" that has repeatedly slipped.

The pattern: a new state's currency succeeds when it is anchored to something credible — a currency board, a peg guaranteed by a larger neighbour, or an independent central bank with foreign reserves — and fails when the new government treats the printing press as revenue. It is the same lesson as Byzantium's, in a world without metal.

## Maastricht, the ERM crisis and Black Wednesday (1992–93)

The Delors Report of 1989 laid out a three-stage path to European monetary union, and the Maastricht Treaty, signed on 7 February 1992 and in force from 1 November 1993, wrote it into law: a European Central Bank modelled on the Bundesbank, with price stability as its sole primary objective and a legal ban on financing governments, and "convergence criteria" — inflation, long-term interest rates, deficits below 3% of GDP, debt below 60% — that members had to meet to join. Britain and Denmark negotiated opt-outs.

The ERM, which fixed European currencies within ±2.25% bands, was supposed to be the bridge. It broke in September 1992. Danish voters rejected Maastricht in June, the Bundesbank's post-reunification rates were far above what Britain and Italy could bear in recession, and speculators — George Soros's Quantum Fund most famously, with a $10 billion short position — bet that sterling and the lira could not be defended. On "Black Wednesday," 16 September 1992, the Bank of England raised rates from 10% to 12% and announced 15% in one day, spent an estimated £3.3 billion of reserves, and withdrew sterling from the ERM that evening; Italy left the next day; Spain, Portugal and Ireland devalued over the following months. In August 1993 the bands were widened to ±15%, which meant the ERM no longer fixed anything. Britain's ejection, ironically, gave it the recovery of the 1990s and confirmed it would never join the euro; it also converted the Bank of England to inflation targeting (October 1992), which led to its operational independence in May 1997.

## The euro (1999–2002)

Despite the crisis, eleven countries met the criteria — with creative accounting in Italy and Belgium and a suspicious pause in Germany — and on 1 January 1999 the euro was born as an accounting currency with irrevocably fixed conversion rates; the ECB, established in June 1998 in Frankfurt, took over monetary policy for 290 million people. Notes and coins arrived on 1 January 2002, and the Deutsche Mark, franc, lira, peseta, guilder and the rest ceased to exist. Greece joined in 2001, and the eurozone reached twenty members with Croatia in 2023 (Bulgaria joined on 1 January 2026, making twenty-one).

The euro was the largest monetary event since Bretton Woods and a deliberate attempt to build a rival reserve currency to the dollar. It succeeded in becoming the second reserve currency (about 20% of allocated reserves, a share that has not grown since the early 2000s) and the second invoicing currency, but not in challenging the dollar, for reasons file 08 of the gold-research set explains: no common bond, no common treasury, no fiscal backstop. Its structural flaw — one monetary policy for economies with different fiscal positions and no mechanism for transfers — was identified at the time by American economists and became the eurozone crisis of 2010–12 (file 07).

## The independence wave

The fiat era's answer to "what disciplines money?" was finalised in this decade. New Zealand's Reserve Bank Act of 1989 gave the bank a single target — inflation of 0–2%, later 1–3% — and the governor could be dismissed for missing it; the first inflation target was announced in 1990. Canada (1991), Britain (1992), Sweden (1993), Australia (1993) and dozens of others followed with explicit targets, usually 2%. Legal independence followed: the Bank of England in 1997, the Bank of Japan under its 1997 law (effective 1998), the ECB by treaty, and the central banks of nearly every emerging market as an IMF-encouraged reform. By 2000 the standard design of a monetary system anywhere on earth was: a fiat currency, an independent central bank, a 2% inflation target, a floating (or credibly pegged) exchange rate. None of this had existed in 1971; all of it was built to replace what gold had done.

## Key takeaways

The end of the Cold War produced some twenty-five new currencies, nearly all of which hyperinflated before stabilising on the Western model of an independent central bank plus a foreign anchor; Estonia's currency board was the success, Yugoslavia's 313-million-per-cent month the catastrophe. German reunification's 1:1 conversion forced Bundesbank rates that broke the European Exchange Rate Mechanism on Black Wednesday 1992, which pushed Britain out for good and pushed the rest of Europe into the euro of 1999. The wave of central-bank independence and 2% inflation targets that began in New Zealand in 1990 became the universal replacement for the discipline gold had once imposed.
