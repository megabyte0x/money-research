# 12 — Glossary

**Bail-in** — Imposing losses on a failing bank's creditors and large depositors rather than on taxpayers; first used in Cyprus (2013), written into EU law in 2014.

**Basel I / II / III** — Successive international bank-capital standards from the Basel Committee: 8% of risk-weighted assets (1988); model-based risk weights (2004); higher capital plus leverage, liquidity and stable-funding ratios (2010–19).

**Brady bonds** — Tradable bonds, partly collateralised by US Treasuries, issued in 1989–94 to replace defaulted Latin American bank loans; origin of the emerging-market bond market.

**Bretton Woods II** — Term (Dooley, Folkerts-Landau, Garber, 2003) for the post-1990s arrangement in which Asian exporters peg cheaply to the dollar, accumulate reserves and lend them back to the US.

**CBDC (central-bank digital currency)** — A digital central-bank liability. Retail access, privacy, remuneration and limits vary by project. The Sand Dollar and e-CNY are named arrangements; as of September 2026 the digital euro remains proposed, with possible 2029 issuance conditional on legislation and an ECB decision. [ECB, project status](https://www.ecb.europa.eu/euro/digital_euro/progress/html/index.en.html).

**Carter Doctrine** — January 1980 US declaration that outside control of the Persian Gulf would be repelled by force; basis of the US military presence in the Gulf.

**Currency board** — A monetary regime in which the local currency is fully backed by and convertible into a foreign anchor currency at a fixed rate, with no independent monetary policy: Hong Kong (1983), Argentina (1991–2002), Estonia (1992–2011), Bulgaria (1997), Bosnia (1998).

**De-dollarisation** — Reduction of the dollar's share in reserves, trade settlement or invoicing; in practice since 2022 mainly a shift of reserves into gold.

**Dollarisation** — A country's adoption of the US dollar as its own currency (Panama, Ecuador 2000, El Salvador 2001, East Timor 2002).

**ERM (Exchange Rate Mechanism)** — The European Monetary System's fixed-band system (1979–98); broke on Black Wednesday, 16 September 1992.

**Eurodollar** — A dollar deposit held in a bank outside the United States; the offshore dollar system centred on London that grew from the 1960s and recycled petrodollars.

**Fiat money** — Money that has no fixed promise to redeem each unit for a specified weight of a commodity. Its purchasing power depends on monetary policy, fiscal and financial institutions, demand and expectations; legal-tender rules can matter but are not a complete value explanation. An issuer may still hold gold or other reserve assets, and countries' transitions away from commodity-linked arrangements occurred at different times.

**GENIUS Act** — US Public Law 119-27, approved 18 July 2025, creates an approval and supervisory framework for *payment stablecoins* as defined in §2(22), rather than approving every existing dollar token. For permitted issuers, §4 requires at least one-to-one identifiable reserves from a specified list that includes currency, Fed balances, demand deposits, short-dated Treasuries, qualifying repos and government money-market funds; a public redemption policy; monthly reserve reporting and accounting-firm examination; and a conditional annual financial-statement audit for certain issuers above $50 billion in outstanding issuance. The tokens are not federally deposit- or share-insured. Under §20, the Act takes effect 18 months after enactment or 120 days after final primary-federal-regulator implementing rules, whichever is earlier; this definition does not establish the present effective date or any named issuer's approval. [Enacted law, §§2–4, 7(e), 20](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/PLAW-119publ27/html/PLAW-119publ27.htm).

**Greenspan put** — Market belief, from October 1987, that the Federal Reserve will cut rates and supply liquidity whenever asset prices fall sharply.

**Herstatt risk** — Settlement risk in a foreign-exchange trade when one currency leg is paid before the other; named for the 1974 failure.

**IEEPA** — International Emergency Economic Powers Act (1977), the US statute used for most sanctions and, in 2025, for tariffs; the Supreme Court ruled in February 2026 that it does not authorise tariffs.

**Impossible trinity (trilemma)** — A country can have at most two of: a fixed exchange rate, free capital movement, and independent monetary policy.

**Inflation targeting** — A monetary-policy framework with an announced numerical inflation objective. New Zealand adopted one in 1990; target levels, measures and institutional rules vary, and many countries use other frameworks. [IMF, adoption table](https://www.imf.org/external/pubs/ft/fandd/basics/pdf/jahpan-inflation-targeting.pdf).

**Jamaica Accords** — IMF agreement of January 1976, implemented through the Second Amendment in force April 1978. Members could choose exchange-rate arrangements other than a gold peg; the amendment removed gold from IMF par values and SDR valuation, abolished its official price and obligatory gold payments to the IMF, but did not end official gold reserves. [IMF Annual Report 1978, “Gold”](https://www.imf.org/external/pubs/ft/ar/archive/pdf/ar1978.pdf).

**Louvre Accord** — February 1987 G6 agreement to stabilise the dollar after the Plaza-induced fall.

**Negative interest rates** — Policy rates below zero, charged on bank reserves; ECB 2014–22, Japan 2016–24, Switzerland, Denmark, Sweden.

**Nixon shock** — The 15 August 1971 package: gold-convertibility suspension, 10% import surcharge, wage-price freeze.

**Petrodollar** — Dollar receipts from oil exports, or the reinvestment and lending of some of those receipts. The 1974 US–Saudi commission supported economic cooperation and petrodollar recycling; it did not establish dollar-for-oil redemption or, on the official record reviewed here, a universal exclusive-pricing rule. [GAO, 1979](https://www.gao.gov/assets/id-79-7.pdf); [Federal Reserve staff memorandum, 1975](https://fraser.stlouisfed.org/title/federal-open-market-committee-meeting-minutes-transcripts-documents-677/meeting-may-20-1975-22476/content/fulltext/FOMC19750502Memo01).

**Plaza Accord** — 22 September 1985 G5 agreement to intervene jointly to lower the dollar.

**Price cap (oil)** — G7/EU measure from December 2022 forbidding Western shipping and insurance services for Russian crude sold above $60 a barrel (lowered in 2025).

**Quantitative easing (QE)** — Central-bank asset purchases paid for with new reserve balances, usually intended to ease financial conditions. A purchase from a non-bank also creates a bank deposit at that step; the seller gives up an asset. Effects depend on the policy setting and are not equivalent to new government borrowing or a customer bank loan. [Bank of England, 2014, Figure 3](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/quarterly-bulletin/2014/money-creation-in-the-modern-economy.pdf).

**Reserve freeze** — Restrictions imposed by sanctioning jurisdictions in 2022 that immobilised Russian central-bank assets held within their reach. Immobilisation limits access to claims; it does not by itself transfer ownership of the underlying principal. Later official gold purchases cannot be assigned a universal reserve-freeze motive without country-level evidence. [Council of the EU, 2024 immobilised-assets mechanism](https://www.consilium.europa.eu/en/press/press-releases/2024/10/23/immobilised-assets-council-greenlights-up-to-35-billion-in-macro-financial-assistance-to-ukraine-and-new-loan-mechanism-implementing-g7-commitment/).

**Ruble zone** — The 1992–93 period in which the fifteen ex-Soviet republics shared the ruble while each could create credit in it; ended by Russia in July 1993.

**Section 122 / 232 / 301** — Provisions of US trade law: 122 (Trade Act 1974) allows a temporary 15% tariff for 150 days for balance-of-payments reasons; 232 (Trade Expansion Act 1962) allows tariffs on national-security grounds; 301 (Trade Act 1974) allows retaliation against unfair trade practices.

**Shock therapy** — Rapid liberalisation, stabilisation and privatisation applied in Bolivia (1985), Poland (1990) and Russia (1992).

**Smithsonian Agreement** — December 1971 G10 realignment (dollar to $38/oz, wider bands) that collapsed in 1973.

**Snake** — 1972 European arrangement fixing EC currencies to each other within ±2.25%.

**Stablecoin** — A token designed to maintain a target value relative to a currency or other asset. Asset-backed and algorithmic designs differ; reserve holdings, holder eligibility and redemption rights must be checked for the named issuer and applicable law. The label alone does not establish cash/Treasury backing or a one-for-one claim available to every holder. [BIS, *The future monetary system*, stablecoin types](https://www.bis.org/publ/arpdf/ar2022e3.pdf).

**Stagflation** — Simultaneous high inflation and high unemployment, first observed in the 1970s.

**Swap lines** — Standing agreements under which the Federal Reserve lends dollars to foreign central banks against their currencies; used at scale in 2008 and 2020.

**SWIFT** — Society for Worldwide Interbank Financial Telecommunication (1973), the bank messaging network; disconnection is a sanction (Iran 2012, Russia 2022).

**Taper tantrum** — The May–June 2013 sell-off in bonds and emerging-market currencies when the Fed hinted it would slow QE.

**TARP** — Troubled Asset Relief Program, the $700 billion US bank-rescue fund of October 2008.

**Volcker shock** — The Fed's 1979–82 shift to reserve targeting and 20% interest rates that ended the Great Inflation at the cost of a double recession.

**Washington Consensus** — John Williamson's 1989 list of ten market-oriented reforms pressed on debtor countries by the IMF, World Bank and US Treasury.

**"Whatever it takes"** — Mario Draghi's 26 July 2012 pledge that the ECB would preserve the euro; effectively made the ECB a lender of last resort to governments.
