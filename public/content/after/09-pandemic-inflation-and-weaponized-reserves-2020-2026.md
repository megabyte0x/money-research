# 09 — Pandemic, Inflation and Weaponised Reserves, 2020–2026: Where the System Stands

## The pandemic response: the largest money creation in history

When COVID-19 shut the world economy in March 2020, governments and central banks did in weeks what had taken years after 2008. The Fed cut rates to zero in two emergency moves (3 and 15 March), announced unlimited bond purchases on 23 March, reopened swap lines with foreign central banks, and for the first time bought corporate bonds; its balance sheet went from $4.2 trillion to nearly $9 trillion by 2022. Congress passed the $2.2 trillion CARES Act on 27 March 2020, the first of about $5 trillion of pandemic spending including direct cheques to households; the deficit reached 15% of GDP in 2020, the highest since 1945. The ECB launched a €750 billion Pandemic Emergency Purchase Programme on 18 March, later enlarged to €1.85 trillion, and in July 2020 the EU agreed to borrow €750 billion jointly for a recovery fund — the first common European debt, the "Hamiltonian moment" the eurozone crisis had failed to produce. Britain, Japan, Canada and Australia did the same in proportion. US broad money grew by about 40% in two years, the fastest since the Second World War.

## The inflation (2021–23)

The result was the first serious inflation in the rich world in forty years. It came from three directions at once: the stimulus, which arrived as supply chains were still closed; the supply chains themselves, with container rates up tenfold and semiconductors unobtainable; and, from February 2022, the Ukraine war's energy shock (file 06). US consumer-price inflation, which the Fed and most economists called "transitory" in 2021, reached 9.1% in June 2022, the highest since 1981; the eurozone's peaked at 10.6% in October 2022 and Britain's at 11.1%; Turkey passed 85% and Argentina 200%. Central banks that had spent a decade trying to raise inflation to 2% now had to crush it. The Fed raised rates from zero in March 2022 to 5.25–5.50% by July 2023, the fastest tightening since Volcker, and began shrinking its balance sheet; the ECB went from −0.5% to 4%; even the Bank of Japan ended negative rates in March 2024 and raised rates for the first time since 2007. The tightening broke things: Britain's gilt market and pension funds nearly collapsed in September 2022 after an unfunded tax-cut budget, forcing the Bank of England to intervene and the prime minister to resign within weeks; Silicon Valley Bank and two other American banks failed in March 2023 in the fastest bank runs in history, conducted by smartphone; and Credit Suisse, a 167-year-old global systemically important bank, was forced into UBS on 19 March 2023 with its AT1 bondholders wiped out. Inflation fell back toward 3% by 2024 without the recession most had predicted, and the Fed cut rates three times in late 2024 and three more in 2025, to 3.50–3.75% by December 2025.

## The weaponisation of reserves and the return of gold (2022–26)

The 2022 freeze of Russian central-bank assets prompted debate over where states hold reserves and who controls access to them; its effect on each central bank's allocation cannot be inferred from aggregate shares alone. The ECB estimated gold at 27% of **official reserves including gold** at end-2025, above the euro (15%) and US Treasuries (22%) in that measure. It attributed most of gold's rise in share to price valuation: holding gold at its end-2023 price would put gold and the euro at roughly 16% each and Treasuries at 26%. [ECB, June 2026, chart 7](https://www.ecb.europa.eu/press/other-publications/ire/html/ecb.ire202606.en.html). Separately, IMF COFER put the dollar at 57.13% of **foreign-exchange reserves** in 2026 Q1. COFER excludes monetary gold, SDRs and IMF reserve positions, and its 2025 Q3 methodological revision replaced the former “allocated/unallocated” split and revised historical observations. The two percentages must not be added, substituted or read as proof that a dollar-share change financed gold purchases. [IMF COFER](https://data.imf.org/Datasets/COFER); [IMF 2026 Q1 data brief](https://data.imf.org/en/news/imf%20data%20brief%20july%201).

## American debt, the Fed and the tariffs (2025–26)

The United States entered this period with a gross national debt that passed $39 trillion in early 2026, deficits of 6–7% of GDP in peacetime and at full employment, and annual interest costs above $1 trillion, more than the defence budget. Moody's removed America's last AAA rating in May 2025. The Trump administration's response combined tax cuts — the One Big Beautiful Bill Act of 4 July 2025, which extended the 2017 cuts and raised the debt ceiling by $5 trillion — with the most aggressive tariffs since the 1930s: a 10% universal tariff and "reciprocal" tariffs of up to 50% announced on 2 April 2025 under the International Emergency Economic Powers Act, plus sectoral tariffs on steel, aluminium, cars and semiconductors. A 43-day government shutdown in October–November 2025 was the longest in history. Trade deals with Britain, Japan, the EU (at 15%) and others followed, and a US–China truce in November 2025 paused the escalation.

On 20 February 2026 the Supreme Court ruled 6–3 that IEEPA does not authorise the president to impose tariffs, invalidating the reciprocal and "trafficking" tariffs at a stroke and opening the way to refunds of up to $175 billion to importers. The administration replaced them within days with a 10% global tariff under Section 122 of the Trade Act of 1974 (which is limited to 150 days) and continued the Section 232 and 301 tariffs, so the effective tariff rate fell but did not return to 2024 levels. The episode matters for money because tariffs had been counted on as revenue against the deficit, and because the courts, not the markets, turned out to be the constraint on executive power over trade.

Pressure on the Federal Reserve was the other constant. Trump attacked chairman Jerome Powell throughout 2025 for not cutting rates, attempted to remove Governor Lisa Cook in August 2025 (which went to litigation), and in May 2026 Kevin Warsh succeeded Powell as chairman. The Fed under Warsh held rates at 3.50–3.75% at its June and July 2026 meetings, with three dissenters in July arguing for a hike, because the Iran war had pushed energy prices up and inflation back to about 3.5% while unemployment stayed near 4.4%; the June projections implied a rate rise rather than a cut by the end of 2026. The independence question — whether a fiat currency's anchor survives when the president chooses the central bank's leadership — is the 1970s question again, and the 2026 energy shock is the 1973 shock again, landing on an economy whose inflation had not fully returned to target.

## Europe, Japan, China and the rest

Europe responded to the Ukraine war and American retrenchment with rearmament and debt: Germany reformed its constitutional debt brake in March 2025 to exempt defence and create a €500 billion infrastructure fund, the EU proposed €800 billion of defence spending, and NATO adopted a 5%-of-GDP target in June 2025. The eurozone's inflation returned to 2% by 2025 and the ECB cut rates to 2%, then held through the 2026 energy shock. Bulgaria adopted the euro on 1 January 2026. Britain, the major economy judged most exposed to the Hormuz closure, faced rising bills and renewed North Sea debate. Japan, with debt over 230% of GDP, raised rates and saw its bond yields reach the highest since the 1990s. China fought deflation and a property collapse (Evergrande's default in 2021 began a slump that had wiped out a large share of household wealth by 2025) with export volumes that produced a trade surplus above $1 trillion in 2025, bought gold every month, and, during the 2026 war, drew on the strategic oil reserves it had spent a decade filling, which cushioned its economy more than any other Asian importer's. Argentina under Milei cut spending, brought inflation down from 200% and received a $20 billion currency swap from the US Treasury in October 2025. The Global South, meanwhile, bore the 2026 energy shock hardest: Bangladesh, Pakistan, Sri Lanka, the Philippines and much of East Africa faced rationing, four-day weeks and unrest.

## The 2026 energy shock

The war on Iran that began in late February 2026 and the closure of the Strait of Hormuz (file 06) produced what the IEA called the largest supply disruption in oil-market history. Brent peaked at $118 on 31 March, fell to $72 by July on ceasefire hopes, and was back near $97 in the first week of September as US–Iran strikes resumed in the strait. The IMF's July 2026 update cut world growth to 3.0% for 2026 and 3.4% for 2027, noting that an investment boom in artificial intelligence was offsetting part of the oil damage. Gold had reached the World Gold Council's USD-series record of $5,405/oz on 29 January, before the war. It tested $4,000 during July, finished that month at $4,027/oz and rebounded to $4,563/oz at end-August. These observations contradict a $4,400 floor throughout the shock. They do not, by themselves, establish how much of the 2022–26 rise came from reserve diversification rather than crisis demand. ## Where money stands in September 2026

The dollar still plays major roles in trade invoicing, foreign-exchange dealing, lending and reserves. Dollar-denominated stablecoins extend some payment uses, but they are not the same thing as central-bank reserves. At end-2025 market prices, gold represented 27% of the ECB's broad official-reserves measure, which includes gold and foreign exchange; its higher share was largely a price effect. The euro and dollar shares in IMF COFER measure the *currency composition of foreign-exchange reserves only*. Comparing these measures requires the denominator, date and valuation convention beside each number. ## Key takeaways

The pandemic and subsequent supply shocks were followed by sharp inflation and monetary tightening. The 2022 reserve freeze renewed attention to gold's custody and issuer-risk properties, but a causal reallocation of reserve portfolios cannot be established from market-value shares alone. At end-2025 gold exceeded both the euro and US Treasuries in the ECB's broad official-reserves measure, largely because its price had risen; the IMF's COFER dollar share refers only to foreign-exchange reserves and excludes gold. Reserve holdings, trade invoicing and payment use answer different questions.
