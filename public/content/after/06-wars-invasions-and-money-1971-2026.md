# 06 — Wars, Invasions and Money, 1971–2026

## How the fiat era changed the financing of war

Under a gold standard a war is paid for by suspending convertibility, borrowing, and inflating; the currency is then painfully restored to gold afterwards (Britain in 1821 and 1925). After 1971 the suspension is permanent, so wars are financed by debt and money creation with no expectation of a return to parity, and the cost shows up as inflation, higher interest rates and a larger national debt rather than as a visible loss of gold. Three other things changed. Oil, priced in dollars and produced in the world's most contested region, transmitted every Middle Eastern war straight into the global price level. Sanctions, which under the gold standard could only be a blockade of goods, became a weapon aimed at money itself — access to dollars, to the SWIFT messaging network, and eventually to a country's own reserves. And the dollar, as the world's safe asset, tended to strengthen in every war, a paradox that lasted until the reserve freeze of 2022 made other states doubt that their dollars were theirs.

## Vietnam and the Yom Kippur War (1965–74)

Vietnam was the war that ended Bretton Woods: financing it without raising taxes produced the dollar overhang that forced the gold window shut (file 01). The Yom Kippur War of October 1973 was the first war of the floating era and gave it its defining shock, the OPEC embargo and the quadrupling of oil (file 02). Together they established the fiat-era pattern: war abroad, inflation at home, and a new role for oil as the dollar's anchor.

## The Soviet–Afghan War and the Iran–Iraq War (1979–89)

The Soviet invasion of Afghanistan in December 1979 and the Iranian Revolution the same year produced the Carter Doctrine, the second oil shock and, indirectly, the Volcker recession (file 02). The Iran–Iraq War (September 1980–August 1988) killed perhaps a million people and, through the "tanker war" of 1984–88, brought the first direct threat to Gulf shipping since 1945; the US Navy reflagged and escorted Kuwaiti tankers in 1987–88. Oil, however, fell during the war as Saudi Arabia flooded the market in 1986, which helped bankrupt the Soviet Union: Soviet hard-currency earnings from oil fell by about a third, the USSR borrowed heavily in the West, and by 1991 it was insolvent. The Afghan war cost the USSR around $50 billion and its army's morale; the Cold War was, in its final phase, lost partly on the oil price.

## The Gulf War (1990–91)

Iraq invaded Kuwait on 2 August 1990, partly over a dispute about Kuwait's overproduction of oil and the $14 billion Iraq owed it from the Iran war. Oil doubled to $40 within two months, which tipped the United States and Britain into the 1990–91 recession. The US-led coalition's campaign (17 January–28 February 1991) cost about $61 billion, of which roughly $54 billion was paid by Kuwait, Saudi Arabia, Japan and Germany — the only major American war of the era that ran a fiscal surplus. The UN sanctions on Iraq that followed (1990–2003) were the most comprehensive ever imposed and included the "oil-for-food" programme from 1996; in November 2000 Saddam Hussein switched Iraq's oil sales from dollars to euros, one of the few explicit challenges to petrodollar pricing, which was reversed within weeks of the 2003 invasion.

## The Yugoslav wars (1991–99)

The wars of Yugoslavia's dissolution — Slovenia 1991, Croatia 1991–95, Bosnia 1992–95, Kosovo 1998–99 — were financed on the Serbian side by pure money printing under UN sanctions, producing the 313-million-per-cent-a-month hyperinflation of January 1994 (file 04). NATO's 78-day bombing of Serbia in 1999 was the first war in Europe since 1945 and the first fought by the alliance; its economic legacy was a set of protectorates (Bosnia, Kosovo) using foreign currency under international administration.

## 9/11, Afghanistan and Iraq (2001–21)

The attacks of 11 September 2001 closed the New York Stock Exchange for four days, the longest since 1933, and the Fed cut rates to 1% over the following two years, feeding the housing bubble (file 07). The invasion of Afghanistan (7 October 2001–August 2021) and Iraq (20 March 2003–2011, with a return in 2014–17 against ISIS) were financed entirely by borrowing: the Bush administration cut taxes in 2001 and 2003 while spending on the wars, and the Costs of War project at Brown University estimated the total cost of the post-9/11 wars, including interest and veterans' care, at about $8 trillion — the first major American wars paid for with no tax increase. The dollar fell about 40% against the euro between 2002 and 2008 as deficits, the current account and oil rose together; oil went from $20 in 2001 to $147 in July 2008, partly on Iraqi supply disruption and mostly on Chinese demand.

The wars also transformed money as a weapon. The USA PATRIOT Act's Title III (October 2001) gave the Treasury power to cut foreign banks off from the dollar system for money-laundering or terrorist-finance concerns, and the Treasury's Office of Foreign Assets Control became a global enforcement agency. The tool was refined against Iran: in March 2012 SWIFT, under EU regulation, disconnected Iranian banks, the first time the network had been used as a sanction, and the resulting 60% fall in Iran's oil exports brought it to the 2015 nuclear agreement. The unintended consequence was that every rival of the United States began building alternatives — China's CIPS payment system (2015), Russia's SPFS and Mir card (2014–15), and bilateral currency swaps — and buying gold.

## Georgia, Crimea and the road to 2022 (2008–21)

Russia's five-day war in Georgia in August 2008 coincided with the financial crisis and passed with few economic consequences. The annexation of Crimea in March 2014 and the war in Donbas brought the first Western sanctions on Russia, targeted at individuals, banks' access to long-term dollar funding, and oil technology; combined with the oil price collapse of 2014–16 (from $110 to $30) they halved the ruble. Russia's response was to "sanction-proof" itself: it paid down foreign debt, built its own card and messaging systems, moved trade with China toward yuan, and, most importantly, sold US Treasuries and bought gold — its reserves went from about 1,000 tonnes in 2013 to over 2,300 by 2020, the fifth-largest hoard in the world. In 2014–18 the Russian central bank was the largest official buyer of gold on earth. This was the first time a major state deliberately restructured its reserves around gold as a defence against the dollar, and it was the template others followed after 2022.

## Ukraine (2022–present)

Russia invaded Ukraine on 24 February 2022. The economic war launched in response was the largest ever mounted against a major economy: within four days the United States, EU, Britain, Japan, Canada and Switzerland froze the Russian central bank's reserves held in their jurisdictions — about $300 billion, roughly half of Russia's total — and removed selected Russian banks from SWIFT; over the following months they banned Russian oil imports, imposed a $60-a-barrel price cap on Russian seaborne crude enforced through Western shipping insurance (December 2022, lowered in 2025), and sanctioned thousands of individuals and companies. Russia responded by cutting gas to Europe, which sent the Dutch TTF benchmark to €340 per megawatt-hour in August 2022 (ten times its 2019 level), produced the European energy crisis of 2022–23 and added several points to eurozone inflation, which peaked at 10.6% in October 2022 (file 09). Russia redirected oil to India and China at a discount through a "shadow fleet" of old tankers, moved most of its trade with China into yuan and rubles, and kept its economy growing on war spending. In 2024 the G7 agreed to lend Ukraine $50 billion serviced by the profits on the frozen Russian assets, and the EU debated outright confiscation, which most central banks opposed for what it would do to the credibility of every reserve currency.

That credibility question is one monetary legacy of the war. The 2022 reserve freezes showed that access to foreign-currency claims can be restricted by their issuing jurisdictions; domestically vaulted gold avoids that particular issuer-access risk but has other custody and liquidity risks. Official gold purchases, which had averaged about 500 tonnes a year in the 2010s, exceeded 1,000 tonnes in 2022, 2023 and 2024. The war itself was still being fought in September 2026: after failed trilateral talks in early 2026, brief Easter and Victory Day truces in April and May, and a US push for a settlement, President Putin said on 3 September 2026 that a deal was possible while Kyiv spoke of a "new dynamic," but no ceasefire was in force.

## Gaza, the Red Sea and the twelve-day war (2023–25)

Hamas's attack on 7 October 2023 and the Israeli war in Gaza that followed had limited direct effect on world markets, but the Houthi attacks on Red Sea shipping from November 2023 cut Suez Canal traffic by about half through 2024, forcing ships around the Cape of Good Hope, raising freight rates and cutting Egypt's canal revenues by about 60%. Israel's strikes on Iran in June 2025, joined by American strikes on the Fordow, Natanz and Isfahan nuclear sites on 22 June, produced a twelve-day war and a brief oil spike but no closure of the Gulf; a Gaza ceasefire was reached in October 2025. Those were the preliminaries.

## The 2026 Iran war and the closure of Hormuz

In the last days of February 2026 the United States and Israel launched a full war on Iran. Iran's answer was to close the Strait of Hormuz, through which about a fifth of the world's oil and a large share of its liquefied natural gas passed, and to strike Gulf energy infrastructure; Qatar declared force majeure on LNG exports on 3 March and an Iranian attack on Ras Laffan on 18 March removed 17% of Qatar's capacity for years. The International Energy Agency called it the largest supply disruption in the history of the oil market and ordered a coordinated release of 400 million barrels from strategic stocks; Japan made the largest release in its history. Brent crude rose from the mid-$60s to a peak of $118 on 31 March, Asian LNG spot prices rose over 140%, jet fuel roughly doubled, and fertiliser supplies (a third of world urea passes through Hormuz) were disrupted. The Philippines declared a national energy emergency, Slovenia became the first EU country to ration fuel, Bangladesh, Sri Lanka, Pakistan, Thailand and Vietnam imposed shortened working weeks and price controls, Kenya and Ethiopia rationed, Ireland's government faced a no-confidence vote over fuel protests, and Spirit Airlines collapsed in May citing fuel costs. The United States, a net energy exporter, saw petrol pass $4 and in California $6 but benefited from record export volumes; China drew on the strategic reserves it had built for exactly this event and halted refined-product exports.

A ceasefire was announced on 8 April 2026 but shipping through the strait did not recover — Iran demanded sanctions relief and reparations before reopening, and by early September only about ten commodity ships a day were transiting, against roughly a hundred before the war. Brent fell back to about $72 by 1 July, rose again to $97 in late July, and was around $97 on 7 September 2026 after a new round of US–Iran strikes in the strait and an attack on Saudi Aramco facilities; Iran was announcing an "exclusion zone" for unauthorised shipping. The IMF's July 2026 update cut world growth to 3.0% for 2026 and noted that the effect of the oil shock had been cushioned by an artificial-intelligence-driven investment boom. The Federal Reserve, the ECB and the Bank of England all held rates through the summer rather than cut, with Fed dissenters arguing for hikes; the parallel with 1973–74 — an energy shock landing on an economy with inflation already above target — was drawn by nearly every commentator (file 09).

## Patterns

Every major war of the fiat era has been inflationary through oil and deficits, and every one has been followed by a rise in gold. The dollar strengthened in every crisis until 2022; since then the world's central banks have hedged their dollars with metal. Sanctions have grown from trade embargoes to the exclusion of whole countries from the payments system and the seizure of their reserves, and each escalation has driven the sanctioned and the merely nervous toward gold, yuan settlement and payment systems outside Western control. And the Persian Gulf, which the Carter Doctrine committed America to defend in 1980, has produced the oil shocks of 1973, 1979, 1990 and 2026 — the last the largest of all.

## Key takeaways

In the fiat era wars are paid for by borrowing and inflation rather than by draining gold, and their cost appears as debt, higher prices and a weaker currency. Oil transmits every Gulf war into the world price level; the 2026 Iran war and the closure of Hormuz produced the largest oil disruption ever recorded. Money itself became a weapon — dollar-system exclusion after 2001, SWIFT cut-offs from 2012, the freezing of Russia's $300 billion in 2022 — and each use pushed the targeted and the watchful toward gold, which is why central banks have bought over 3,500 tonnes since 2022.
