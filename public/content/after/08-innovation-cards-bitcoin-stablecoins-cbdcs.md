# 08 — Innovation: How Money Dematerialised, 1971–2026

## Why 1971 was also a technological turning point

The year the dollar left gold was also the year the Intel 4004 microprocessor shipped (November 1971), the Nasdaq opened as the world's first electronic stock market (February 1971), and the first money-market fund appeared. This is not a coincidence in the loose sense: fiat money is information, and the fifty years since 1971 have been the fifty years in which information became cheap to move. A gold coin has to be carried; a dollar that is only a promise can be a ledger entry, a magnetic stripe, a packet on a network or a token on a blockchain. Almost every financial innovation since 1971 is either a way of managing the risks that floating currencies and rates created, or a way of moving fiat money faster and more cheaply, or, from 2009, an attempt to build money that needs no issuer at all.

## Managing the new risks: derivatives (1972–2000)

Floating rates created price risk that had not existed under Bretton Woods, and the derivatives industry grew to hedge it. Currency futures began at the Chicago Mercantile Exchange in May 1972 (Leo Melamed, advised by Milton Friedman, who was paid $7,500 for a supporting paper); listed stock options at the Chicago Board Options Exchange in April 1973, the month Fischer Black and Myron Scholes published their pricing formula; Treasury futures in 1977; stock-index futures in 1982; and the first interest-rate swap between IBM and the World Bank in 1981. The International Swaps and Derivatives Association (1985) standardised the contracts. JPMorgan's team invented the credit default swap in 1994 and popularised value-at-risk models; collateralised debt obligations followed. By 2008 the notional value of over-the-counter derivatives was $600 trillion, ten times world GDP. Portfolio insurance caused the 1987 crash, LTCM's swaps nearly caused one in 1998, and credit default swaps on subprime mortgages caused the real one in 2008 — each crisis a derivative of the instruments invented to manage the risks of fiat money.

## Moving money: from SWIFT to real-time payments (1973–2023)

The plumbing of fiat money was built in the 1970s. CHIPS, the New York dollar clearing system, opened in 1970; SWIFT, the bank messaging cooperative in Brussels, was founded in 1973 and went live in 1977, replacing telex — it now carries about 50 million messages a day and, since 2012, has been a sanctions weapon (file 06). Fedwire and the other central-bank settlement systems went electronic. BankAmericard became Visa in 1976 and Master Charge became MasterCard in 1979, and the magnetic-stripe card, the ATM (Barclays, 1967; widespread by the 1980s) and the point-of-sale terminal moved retail payments off paper. Bloomberg's terminal (1982) and electronic trading through the 1990s (Reg NMS in the US, 2005) made markets continuous and global; by the 2010s most equity volume was algorithmic and high-frequency trading measured latency in microseconds. The internet added online banking, Amazon (1994) and PayPal (1998); the smartphone (2007) added mobile money — Kenya's M-Pesa (2007) let people with no bank account send money by text, and China's Alipay (2004) and WeChat Pay (2013) leapfrogged cards entirely so that by 2020 China was nearly cashless. India's Unified Payments Interface (2016) and Brazil's Pix (November 2020) built state-run instant systems that process billions of transactions a month for free; the US Federal Reserve's FedNow arrived only in July 2023. Cash's share of transactions in most rich countries fell below 20%. The physical form of fiat money, the banknote, is now a minority medium.

## Financialising gold: the ETF (2003–04)

Gold's own form changed. Gold exchange-traded funds — Australia's Gold Bullion Securities in 2003 and, decisively, SPDR Gold Shares (GLD) in New York in November 2004 — let anyone buy an ounce of vaulted gold as a stock-exchange security for a fee of 0.4% a year. GLD became the fastest-growing fund in history and at times the largest private holder of gold on earth; ETFs held over 3,000 tonnes by 2020 and bought about 800 tonnes in 2025 alone. The ETF turned gold from a thing you had to store into a line in a brokerage account, which is exactly what banknotes had once done for it, and it made Western investment demand a swing factor in the price alongside Asian jewellery and central banks.

## Money without an issuer: Bitcoin (2008–)

Satoshi Nakamoto published the Bitcoin whitepaper on 31 October 2008, and the network began in January 2009. It offers a publicly verifiable issuance schedule and ledger without a central issuer; confidence in a payment grows with confirmations rather than becoming absolutely irreversible. Exchanges, custodians and payment layers later developed around that base system. El Salvador's 2021 law required Bitcoin acceptance alongside the U.S. dollar. **Decree 199, adopted in January 2025 and effective 90 days after publication, amended rather than repealed that law:** private acceptance became voluntary, Bitcoin tax-payment authority was removed, and the domestic text retained “curso legal” wording. [Decree 199, arts. 1–8](https://www.jurisprudencia.gob.sv/DocumentosBoveda/R/2/2020-2029/2021/06/1080B6.HTML?embedded=true). The IMF calls this removal of the essential features of legal tender. [IMF Country Report 25/58, para. 27 n.18](https://www.imf.org/-/media/files/publications/cr/2025/english/1slvea2025001-print-pdf.pdf). These changes did not make Bitcoin the general unit of prices or wages.

Whether Bitcoin is money is still argued. It is not a unit of account (nothing is priced in it), it is a poor medium of exchange (slow, volatile, taxed as property), but it has functioned for sixteen years as a store of value that no state controls, which is the one job gold does that fiat cannot. It is best understood as the fiat era's attempt to rebuild the properties of gold in software, and the fact that it was invented in the month the banks were bailed out says what its inventor thought of fiat.

## The digital dollar by accident: stablecoins (2014–)

The innovation that has actually changed the monetary system is the stablecoin: a token on a blockchain redeemable one-for-one for a dollar, backed by dollars and Treasury bills held by the issuer. Tether (2014) and Circle's USDC (2018) began as plumbing for crypto exchanges and became a global dollar payment rail: by mid-2026 the stablecoin market was about $320 billion, over 99% of it in dollars, processing more transaction volume than Visa and roughly half of America's ACH system, with Tether alone holding over $125 billion of Treasuries — more than most countries — and, the ECB reported, buying over 100 tonnes of gold in 2025 as an additional reserve. The GENIUS Act, signed on 18 July 2025, gave the United States its first federal stablecoin law: issuers must hold 100% reserves in cash and short-term Treasuries, be licensed and audited, and comply with anti-money-laundering rules; the Treasury can ban non-compliant foreign coins. The broader market-structure bill, the CLARITY Act, passed the House in July 2025 and was still moving through the Senate in 2026. The Treasury's stated purpose is explicit: stablecoins extend dollar access to billions of people and create a structural buyer of US debt. In other words, the private sector has built, and the state has now licensed, a digital version of the Eurodollar system — dollars circulating outside banks, backed by the same Treasuries that backed the petrodollar. Stablecoins are the strongest evidence that the dollar's network effect survives the end of gold, sanctions and de-dollarisation: even the money invented to escape the state ended up denominated in its currency.

## The state's answer: central-bank digital currencies (2020–)

Facebook's announcement of Libra in June 2019 — a private global currency backed by a basket of assets, available to 2.4 billion users — alarmed every central bank and was regulated to death by 2022, but it started the CBDC race. The Bahamas issued the first, the Sand Dollar, in October 2020; Nigeria's eNaira (2021) failed to attract users; China's e-CNY, piloted from 2020, had processed trillions of yuan in transactions by 2025 but remained a small share of Chinese payments, which Alipay and WeChat already dominated. The Bank for International Settlements' Project mBridge (China, Hong Kong, Thailand, UAE, Saudi Arabia) built a platform for settling cross-border payments in CBDCs without SWIFT or the dollar — the BIS withdrew from it in late 2024 as it became a sanctions-evasion concern. The European Central Bank finished the core technology of a digital euro; the European Parliament adopted its negotiating position in 2026, and under the ECB's plan a pilot could begin in mid-2027 with first issuance in 2029, with holdings capped at about €3,000 to protect bank deposits. The United States, by contrast, banned a retail CBDC by executive order in January 2025 and chose the private stablecoin route instead. India, Brazil, Russia and about 130 other countries have projects at some stage.

A CBDC is a direct claim on the central bank in digital form — a banknote on a phone — and it raises the questions the fiat era has spent fifty years avoiding: if money is a state ledger entry, the state can see every transaction, can pay negative interest on it, and can switch it off. The design fights over privacy and holding limits are, at bottom, fights about what kind of money a fiat system should be.

## Innovation's effect on the monetary system

The cumulative result of these fifty years is that money is now almost entirely information: the world's roughly $100 trillion of broad money exists as ledger entries, and its physical component is a few trillion in notes. That has made money faster and cheaper than at any point in history, has made it easier for the state to create (QE is a keystroke), to trace and to seize, and has made it possible for private parties to build both dollar substitutes (stablecoins) and dollar alternatives (Bitcoin). Gold, the only monetary asset that is still a physical thing, has been carried into the digital world by the ETF while retaining the property that makes it valuable to central banks in 2026: it exists without a ledger and can be held without anyone's permission.

## Key takeaways

Floating rates created the derivatives industry; cheap computing created electronic markets and payment rails that made fiat money a ledger entry; and the 2008 bailouts created Bitcoin, an attempt to rebuild gold in software. The innovation that actually reshaped the system was the dollar stablecoin, which by 2026 had become a $320 billion private Eurodollar market licensed by the GENIUS Act and backed by Treasuries — extending the dollar's reach rather than escaping it. Central banks answered with digital currencies of their own, of which China's is the largest and the digital euro the most advanced in the West, while the United States chose private stablecoins over a state coin. Gold entered the digital age through the ETF without losing the physical independence that is its point.
