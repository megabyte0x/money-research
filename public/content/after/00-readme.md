# After Gold: The Fiat World, 1971–2026 — Research Directory

Companion to the `gold-research/` set. That directory ends on 15 August 1971, when the United States closed the gold window and, for the first time in history, no currency on earth was defined as a weight of anything. This directory asks what happened next: how a world with no metallic anchor organised its money, what rules and treaties replaced the gold standard, how wars were financed and fought with money itself as a weapon, what new nations and new currencies were born, what technologies changed the form of money, and how each of these shocks moved the world economy.

Compiled 8 September 2026. Historical material is drawn from standard economic-history sources; present-day figures come from the IMF, the ECB, the World Gold Council, central-bank statements and press reports current to the first week of September 2026. Every file ends with a "Key takeaways" section so the arc can be skimmed by reading only those.

## The arc in one paragraph

Nixon suspended dollar–gold convertibility in August 1971, and major currencies began floating against the dollar in 1973. The IMF's Second Amendment took effect in 1978. These changes reshaped the constraints on monetary policy, but they did not *start* the Great Inflation: US inflation had been rising since the mid-1960s. Policy choices, expectations and oil shocks also contributed. [Federal Reserve History, “The Great Inflation”](https://www.federalreservehistory.org/essays/great-inflation).

This volume follows the shift to floating rates, the inflation and disinflation of the 1970s and early 1980s, debt crises, new currencies, financial globalisation, the 2008 crisis, and newer payment and reserve arrangements. The dollar retained a central role in invoicing, banking and reserves. Oil was widely invoiced in dollars and exporters invested some proceeds in dollar assets, but dollars were not redeemable for oil. Alongside new technology and markets came new exposures and rules; the chapters distinguish a payment message from settlement, bank deposits from central-bank money, and a dated observation from a durable monetary mechanism. The recent events and figures discussed here require source-by-source review before they can support a definitive verdict.
