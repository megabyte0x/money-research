# 09 — Supply and Control: Who Holds Bitcoin, Who Runs the Network, and Who Benefits
 
## Two different kinds of control
 
Bitcoin's supply is controlled by no one and its market is controlled by a few. These are different facts and the difference is the key to the whole subject. The *rules* — 21 million coins, the halving schedule, what counts as a valid block — are enforced by every one of the roughly 23,000 reachable nodes that validate the chain, and no holder, miner, developer or government can change them without persuading the rest to follow. The *market* — the dollar price, liquidity, custody, the construction of blocks — is concentrated in a handful of firms and one state, to a degree that would alarm a competition regulator in any other industry. Owning bitcoin gives no vote over the protocol; Satoshi's 1.1 million coins, BlackRock's 772,000 and Strategy's 845,000 have exactly the same protocol power as zero. What ownership and infrastructure give is influence over the price, the flows, and the choke points through which most people hold the asset.
 
## The supply, September 2026
 
At block 966,780 on 13 September 2026 about 20.08 million bitcoins had been issued — 95.6% of the cap — with roughly 920,000 left to mine over the next century. The block subsidy has been 3.125 coins since the fourth halving on 19–20 April 2024; the fifth, at block 1,050,000, is expected around 16–19 April 2028 and will cut it to 1.5625. Issuance is 450 coins a day, about 164,000 a year, an inflation rate of 0.8% falling to 0.4% in 2028. Transaction fees, the other source of miner income, were 0.4–0.7% of block rewards through 2026 — a ten-year low, and the reason file 11 treats the security budget as a structural defect.
 
The circulating supply is smaller than the issued supply. Between 2.3 and 4 million coins are estimated permanently lost (Chainalysis, River, Unchained), including the roughly 1.1 million mined by Satoshi in 2009–10 that have never moved. About 14.5 million coins had not moved for five months or more in March 2026; roughly 11 million, 56% of supply, sat outside active trade in ETFs, corporate treasuries, custody and dormant wallets in July 2026; and coins ageing into ten-year dormancy (566 a day) now exceed new issuance (450 a day). Coins on exchanges fell to a seven-year low — 1.3 to 2.7 million depending on the counting method — down from over 3.2 million in 2023–24, as they moved into ETF vaults and cold storage. The effective liquid supply is perhaps 3–5 million coins against an issued 20 million, which is why flows of a few billion dollars move the price by tens of percent.
 
## Who holds it
 
**Exchange-traded funds.** The US spot ETFs held 1.251 million coins ($96.6 billion) on 11 September 2026 — about 6.2% of supply — of which BlackRock's IBIT held 771,610 (3.8% of supply on its own), Fidelity's FBTC 175,230 and Grayscale's GBTC 129,360. Coinbase is custodian for nine of the eleven funds and 81–84% of their assets; Fidelity custodies its own. Aggregate ETF holdings were flat between December 2024 and September 2026: the funds absorbed 2025's inflows and 2026's outflows (record daily redemptions of $528 million on 30 January and 27 May 2026, a nine-session $2.8 billion run in late May, a single $1.29 billion block sale of IBIT shares on 26 May) and ended where they began.
 
**Corporate treasuries.** About 190 public companies hold roughly 1.25 million coins, 6% of supply — but one company holds two-thirds of that. Strategy (the renamed MicroStrategy) held 845,050 coins on 31 August 2026, bought for $63.7 billion at an average of about $75,400, financed by selling common stock at a premium to the value of its bitcoin, zero-coupon convertible notes, and four series of perpetual preferred stock paying 8–12%. Its "infinite money glitch" — sell shares above net asset value, buy bitcoin, raise bitcoin per share, repeat — worked while the premium lasted. In 2026 the premium went: its shares fell from $417 to $82, its market value dropped to roughly the value of its coins, and in June 2026 it adopted a new framework that included the first bitcoin sales since 2022 (6,948 coins by early September) to fund $1.26 billion a year of preferred dividends. After Strategy come Twenty One Capital (43,500 coins; created by Tether, Bitfinex, SoftBank and Cantor Fitzgerald, now controlled by Tether), Japan's Metaplanet (43,000), the miner MARA (35,600), Strive, Galaxy, Bullish, SpaceX (18,700), Coinbase (16,500) and Trump Media (12,000, which abandoned the strategy in August 2026 after $190 million of paper losses). GameStop, which bought 4,710 coins in 2025, appears to have sold them.
 
**Governments.** Thirteen states hold about 650,000 coins, 3% of supply, almost none of it bought. The United States holds about 328,000 (198,000 finally forfeited plus 127,000 from the October 2025 Prince Group case) under the March 2025 executive order; China nominally 190,000 from the PlusToken seizure, most of which court records suggest was sold in 2019–20; the United Kingdom 61,000 awaiting a court decision; El Salvador 7,762; the UAE 6,420 mined; Bhutan a few hundred to a few thousand after selling most of a 13,295 peak; Kazakhstan 3,500. The "46,000" attributed to Ukraine are officials' declared private holdings. North Korea's 800 labelled coins understate the $2 billion it stole in 2025 and laundered.
 
**Stablecoin issuers and exchanges.** Tether held 98,932 coins on its June 2026 balance sheet ($5.8 billion), beside $18.8 billion of gold and $115 billion of Treasuries; its chief executive claims 130,000–140,000 across affiliates plus control of Twenty One. Wallets labelled to Coinbase hold about 982,000 coins (its own, clients' and the ETFs'), Binance about 705,000, Robinhood 141,000, Bitfinex 130,000. The Mt. Gox estate still held about 35,000 coins in late 2025, with a court deadline of 31 October 2026 for distribution.
 
**Concentration.** Address-level statistics ("2% of addresses hold 95%") are misleading because the largest addresses are exchange and ETF vaults holding coins for millions of people. Entity-adjusted, Glassnode estimates that entities with over 1,000 coins hold about 31% of supply and that the top 2% of entities hold about 71%. That is roughly the concentration of global financial wealth. What is new in 2026 is *institutional* concentration: about 8–9 million coins — 40–45% of supply — sit with identifiable institutions and states, and two vehicles, IBIT and Strategy, hold 8% between them.
 
## Who runs the network
 
**Mining.** The network's hashrate was about 950 exahashes per second in September 2026, down from a peak above one zettahash in the autumn of 2025 as listed miners moved power to AI hosting. Mining pools, which combine individual miners' machines and construct the blocks, are concentrated: over the week to 13 September 2026 Foundry USA (owned by Digital Currency Group) produced 25.5% of blocks, AntPool (owned by the Chinese hardware maker Bitmain) 18.5%, F2Pool 13.5%, ViaBTC 9.1% and SpiderPool 9.0% — the top two 44%, the top five 76%. The number of entities needed to control a majority of blocks is three. Worse, an April 2025 analysis by the developer 0xB10C showed that nine other pools — Poolin, BTC.com, Braiins, Binance Pool, Luxor and others — were relaying AntPool's block templates rather than building their own, so that "AntPool and friends" controlled 30–40% of hashrate and six template producers mined over 95% of blocks. Bitmain makes about 82% of the world's mining chips, and the top three makers over 99%. The geography is American: the United States hosts 37% of hashrate (Russia 17%, China, where mining is illegal, 12%). In May 2026 pools with 75% of hashrate pledged to adopt Stratum V2, which lets individual miners build their own templates; the pledge is not yet measured.
 
Concentration in mining is a censorship and reorganisation risk, not an issuance risk. A majority of hashrate could refuse to include certain transactions or, at great cost, reverse recent blocks; it could not create a coin the nodes would accept. The cost of assembling a majority from scratch is about $5 billion of hardware plus gigawatts of power, and an attacker would destroy the value of the asset it attacked. The realistic threat is a state coercing two or three pool operators, or Bitmain's firmware — which is why the American domicile of Foundry and the US tariff regime that Bitmain now builds factories to avoid matter.
 
**Development.** Bitcoin Core, the reference software, has about 41 active contributors and five maintainers with the right to merge code. Their funding is under $10 million a year, from Brink, Chaincode, Spiral (Jack Dorsey's Block), OpenSats (about 90% funded by Dorsey), the Human Rights Foundation, MIT and a few exchanges; the "Dorsey problem" — one donor behind most of the grant channels — is the main concentration in this layer. Changes to consensus rules are proposed as BIPs and deployed as soft forks that need adoption by the economic nodes and, in practice, signalling by miners. The precedent for who decides was set in 2017, when miners and large companies backed a block-size increase (SegWit2x, the "New York Agreement") and node operators threatened a user-activated fork that would orphan non-compliant blocks; the miners' proposal was cancelled for lack of consensus. The lesson: developers write code, miners choose among valid blocks, and users' nodes decide what is valid. The 2025–26 dispute over Bitcoin Core version 30, which raised the limit on arbitrary data in transactions from 83 bytes to 100 kilobytes, drove a fifth to a quarter of nodes to a rival client, Knots, maintained by essentially one person — a demonstration that the community can defect from Core, and of how few people set policy.
 
**The next test.** The quantum migration will be the first consensus change in which the largest holders have a direct financial stake in the outcome. BIP-360 (a quantum-resistant address type) does nothing for coins in old exposed-key addresses; BIP-361 (April 2026) proposes to block sends to vulnerable address types three years after activation and to invalidate legacy signatures after five, which would freeze Satoshi's coins and every other unmigrated holding — an estimated third of supply. ETF sponsors, Strategy and custodians, who now hold 15% of supply, will have views; the process has no way to weigh them, and no deadline it can enforce.
 
## Corporations that benefit from control of supply or flows
 
The asset's supply cannot be manipulated; its price and its plumbing can be, and have been.
 
**The evidence of manipulation.** John Griffin and Amin Shams's *Journal of Finance* paper "Is Bitcoin Really Un-Tethered?" (2020) found that Tether issuance in 2017 was timed after price falls, flowed through a single Bitfinex-linked wallet and clustered below round-number prices, and was large enough to explain much of that year's rise to $20,000. New York's attorney general settled with Tether and Bitfinex in February 2021 ($18.5 million) after finding that Tether had at times no banking anywhere and had used its reserves to cover $850 million of Bitfinex's losses; the CFTC fined them $42.5 million in October 2021, finding Tether was fully backed on only 27.6% of days in a 26-month sample. Mt. Gox's "Willy" bot bought about 270,000 coins with non-existent dollars during the 2013 run to $1,100. Bitwise's 2019 presentation to the SEC found 95% of reported bitcoin volume was fake; Forbes still found 51% fake in 2022. The SEC refused spot ETFs for six years on manipulation grounds and approved them in 2024 only because a court ruled it could not distinguish spot from futures products surveilled through CME — the manipulation concern was resolved by surveillance-sharing, not by cleaning up offshore markets, where perpetual futures in Tether's dollar still set the reference price.
 
**Paper bitcoin.** Claims on bitcoin can exceed bitcoin. Exchanges and lenders did this repeatedly — Mt. Gox in 2014, FTX with its $8 billion customer shortfall in 2022, Celsius "lending the same assets over and over" — and proof-of-reserves snapshots do not detect it because they omit liabilities. The ETFs create and redeem in cash, so their shares cannot exceed vaulted coins, but 81–84% of those coins are at one custodian, Coinbase, which lost $360 million in the second quarter of 2026. Offshore derivatives and bitcoin-backed loans remain the main channels of synthetic supply.
 
**The structural beneficiaries.** *Strategy* profits from a premium on its shares that only persists while bitcoin rises and capital markets stay open; below a premium of one, each share sale dilutes, and each dividend is paid from coin sales. *ETF sponsors* collect $350–400 million a year in fees whichever way the price goes and gain agenda-setting weight (BlackRock's prospectus warning on quantum risk, in 2025, moved the debate). *Coinbase* earns custody and trading fees and a share of USDC interest. *Tether* earns about $5 billion a year on Treasuries, holds 100,000 coins and controls a mining-and-treasury conglomerate; Cantor Fitzgerald custodies most of its reserves, took a stake in it and sponsored Twenty One's listing, and Cantor's former chairman, Howard Lutnick, became US commerce secretary in February 2025 and handed the firm to his sons. *Bitmain and AntPool* sit on both the supply of hashpower and the construction of blocks. *Market makers* (Jump, Wintermute, Cumberland, Galaxy) provide most ETF liquidity, which is why a single block trade moves the tape. *The Trump family's ventures* — World Liberty Financial (60% owned by a Trump entity, about $1 billion of profits by December 2025, whose stablecoin carried a $2 billion Abu Dhabi investment into Binance before Binance's founder was pardoned), American Bitcoin (a miner 20% owned by the family, buying Bitmain rigs while the administration set tariffs on them), and Trump Media's bitcoin treasury — mean that the executive branch managing a 328,000-coin reserve, licensing stablecoins and setting mining tariffs is economically exposed to all three.
 
**What none of them can do.** No entity can change the cap, the schedule or the validity rules. A hard fork that raised issuance would create a minority coin, as Bitcoin Cash did in 2017. The forms of control that remain are indirect: over the dollar price (Tether issuance, ETF flows, a few market makers on a few venues), over custody (Coinbase and Binance as two choke points for seizure or instruction), over block construction (three pools, six template producers, one chip maker), over the direction of development (five maintainers, one dominant donor, and the coming quantum decision), and over the whole market by the state that holds 1.6% of supply, hosts 37% of hashrate, regulates the ETFs and licenses the stablecoin. That state has more leverage over bitcoin-the-market than any private firm — and still none over bitcoin-the-rules.
 
## Key takeaways
 
About 20.08 million of 21 million bitcoins exist, 450 a day are added until the 2028 halving, 2–4 million are lost, and the liquid float may be only 3–5 million — which is why flows of a few billion dollars move the price by tens of percent. Ownership is concentrated in ways that were unimaginable in 2019: ETFs hold 6% of supply, mostly at one custodian; one company, Strategy, holds 4% on leverage it can no longer easily service; thirteen governments hold 3%, almost all seized; and roughly 40% of supply sits with identifiable institutions and states. The network's rules are controlled by no one — coin ownership confers no vote, and a fork that changed issuance would create a minority coin — but its market is controlled by few: three pools produce a majority of blocks, one chip maker supplies 82% of hardware, five maintainers merge the code, and Tether's dollar prices the offshore markets where the reference price is set. Documented manipulation (Tether in 2017, Mt. Gox's bot, fake volume) worked through those choke points, and the firms that profit structurally — Strategy, the ETF sponsors, Coinbase, Tether and Cantor, Bitmain, the market makers, and the ventures of the US president's family — profit from flows and premiums, not from the supply, which they cannot touch.
