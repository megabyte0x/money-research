# Bitcoin: Money Without an Issuer, 2008–2026 — Research Directory
 
Third volume of the money project. `gold-research/` traced gold from ornament to the standard that ended on 15 August 1971; the After Gold set followed the fiat world from that day to September 2026. This directory takes the one innovation of the fiat era that was built to escape it — bitcoin, released six weeks after Lehman as a deliberate negative image of the bailout — and asks the questions the project's end goal requires: what currency structure it created, what it solved against fiat in real use, what it would take for a country to adopt it, how a fixed-supply money would work as a unit of account (fixed rate, variable rate, backing fiat, and how denomination would shift), which countries have adopted it and what happened, who favours and opposes it and why, who holds and controls the supply and who profits from that, what path a large economy would follow, what defects keep it from being a global currency, which parts of it can reproduce the old crises, why a restored standard would (or would not) be bitcoin rather than gold — and whether it is the answer to money after gold.
 
Compiled 13 September 2026. Present-day figures — supply, holdings, prices, hashrate, ETF assets, country positions — are from trackers, central-bank statements, IMF documents and press reports current to 11–13 September 2026 and are snapshots that will move. Every topic file ends with a "Key takeaways" section so the argument can be followed by reading only those.

## The arc in one paragraph
 
Bitcoin was published six weeks after Lehman failed and its first block carried the headline of the bank bailout; it was designed as gold rebuilt in software — a supply of 21 million that anyone can audit, settlement that no one can reverse, ownership by whoever holds the key — and its one genuinely new idea was to keep a ledger without anyone in charge. In seventeen years it has become a $1.5 trillion asset held by 365 million people, three ETF sponsors, one software company with 845,000 coins and a dozen governments, and it has delivered, in the real world, what it promised to individuals who distrust institutions: finality, an auditable supply, and money that cannot be frozen so long as it is self-custodied. It has not become money. Under 2% of its users spend it, nothing is priced in it, its scaling layer is shrinking and carrying dollars, and the one country that made it legal tender — El Salvador, with a mandate and a subsidy — saw usage fall to 8% of the population and repealed the law under IMF conditions; the Central African Republic lasted a year. The reasons are structural and match, point for point, the reasons the gold standard ended: a supply that cannot expand in a panic, deflation in a growing economy, no lender of last resort, and a democracy's refusal to subordinate employment to a fixed base — to which bitcoin adds a vertical supply curve that makes volatility permanent, a security budget that is 99% subsidy and halves in 2028, a governance process that cannot enforce a deadline against a quantum threat to a third of supply, and a dependency on the dollar so deep that its own price, settlement and payment rails run on dollar stablecoins licensed by the US Treasury. The market fixed every defect it could — legal status, accounting, custody, access — and each fix reintroduced an intermediary and moved bitcoin further inside the dollar system, so that by 2026 the money invented to escape the state is held through ETFs at one custodian, priced in Tether's dollar, and stockpiled by the US government from confiscations while its president's family runs mining, stablecoin and treasury businesses. Central banks, needing an unfreezable reserve after Russia's assets were seized in 2022, chose gold — a thousand tonnes a year, now the largest reserve asset after the dollar — and one small central bank bought $1 million of bitcoin as an experiment. Bitcoin is the first non-state reserve asset since gold and a hedge against slow debasement that is sold first in every fast crisis; a bitcoin standard would require reversing the political conditions that ended gold and solving problems gold never had; and the realistic path for large economies ends with bitcoin as a small line in the reserve portfolio beside a much larger line of gold, measured in dollars.
