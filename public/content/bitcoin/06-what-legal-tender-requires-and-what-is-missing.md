# 06 — What Legal Tender Requires, and What Bitcoin Still Lacks
 
## What "legal tender" actually means
 
Legal tender is a narrow legal status, not a synonym for money. It means that a creditor must accept the asset in discharge of a debt, that the state accepts it in payment of taxes, and that courts enforce contracts denominated in it. It does not mean that anyone is required to price goods in it, that shops must take it for new sales, or that the population will use it. The dollar is legal tender in the United States and most transactions there are bank deposits, which are not. When El Salvador made bitcoin legal tender in 2021 it went further than the term requires — mandatory acceptance for sales, not only for debts — and when it reversed in 2025 it fell back to "voluntary payment method," which is where bitcoin sits everywhere else in the world that has not banned it.
 
For a country to adopt bitcoin as legal tender, and more so as a monetary standard, a specific list of things must exist or be changed. The list below is drawn from the IMF's 2023 framework, the two experiments, and the statements of the central banks in file 05. Bitcoin, as of 2026, fails most of it.
 
## The legal architecture
 
**Monetary law.** Every country's central-bank act or currency law defines the unit of account, the legal-tender instruments and who may issue them. Adding bitcoin requires amending that law, defining how bitcoin debts are discharged (at a fixed rate or at spot, and whose spot), and giving courts a valuation rule. El Salvador could do this in one law because it had no domestic currency to protect; the dollar had been the only legal tender since 2001, so there was no seigniorage to lose and no central-bank balance sheet to defend. This is why the only two experiments were in dollarised or CFA-franc economies — and why the CFA one failed at once.
 
**Monetary-union treaties.** A member of a currency union cannot act alone. The Central African Republic's law conflicted with the CEMAC treaty that gives the regional central bank sole authority over legal tender; the union's heads of state reaffirmed the monopoly and the law was repealed within a year. The same constraint binds the eurozone (the treaty makes euro banknotes the only legal tender), the West African franc zone, and the Eastern Caribbean Currency Union.
 
**Central-bank reserve statutes.** Reserve assets are enumerated in law. Powell: "The Federal Reserve Act says what we can own." Japan's government: the legal framework "does not recognise Bitcoin as part of foreign exchange reserves." The Czech central bank avoided this by booking its purchase as an intangible asset outside reserves. Holding bitcoin in reserves therefore requires legislation, not a board vote, and legislation can be reversed by the next government — which is why the Bundesbank and the ECB warn against a "government seal of approval" that the public would read as permanent.
 
**IMF conditions.** The IMF's first policy element is not to grant legal-tender status, and its second is that central banks should not hold unbacked crypto as reserves. Any country with an IMF programme — El Salvador, Pakistan, Argentina, most of Sub-Saharan Africa — should expect the conditions El Salvador received: voluntary acceptance, taxes in fiat, no public accumulation, no bitcoin-linked debt. Article IV surveillance applies the same pressure to countries without programmes.
 
## The financial architecture
 
**Bank regulation.** The Basel standard in force since January 2025 assigns bitcoin a 1,250% risk weight and caps exposure at 1–2% of Tier 1 capital. A bank in a bitcoin-standard economy could not hold its own currency without capital equal to the holding. Either the standard is carved out — which the Basel Committee has shown no inclination to do, though the Bitcoin Policy Institute calls the weight "a category error" and a review is under way — or the payment system must run outside banks.
 
**Accounting.** US companies could only carry bitcoin at fair value from 2025 (FASB's 2023 standard); under IFRS it is still an intangible asset or, for brokers, inventory. Public-sector accounting standards have no category for it. A state that holds bitcoin books it as "other assets," which is what the US and El Salvador do, and marks it wherever it chooses.
 
**Tax.** Legal tender in one country does not change tax law in another, and it did not change it at home: the IMF required El Salvador to collect taxes in dollars. In the United States, bitcoin is property, so every purchase is a taxable disposal at its dollar value — "regardless of the amount," in the IRS's words; a de minimis exemption for small transactions has been proposed and not enacted. India's 30% rate and 1% withholding suppress use by design. So long as the tax unit of account is fiat, every bitcoin payment is a fiat transaction with extra paperwork, and fiat remains the unit of account by law.
 
**Anti-money-laundering rules.** The FATF's Travel Rule requires exchanges and custodians to identify senders and receivers; 99 jurisdictions have it or are adopting it, and non-compliance means grey-listing. A bitcoin-standard economy would need licensed, supervised intermediaries — which is to say, the banking system it was meant to bypass — or accept exclusion from the correspondent-banking network.
 
## The economic requirements
 
**Price stability.** Nothing else on the list matters if the unit moves 30–45% a year. Wages, rents, loans and pensions cannot be set in an asset that lost half its value between October 2025 and June 2026. The classical threshold, drawn from gold and the major currencies, is annualised volatility somewhere under 10%; bitcoin's lowest year on record, 2025, was 23%. This is the binding constraint, and there is no evidence it is close to being met.
 
**Elasticity and a lender of last resort.** A modern economy's money supply expands in a panic because a central bank lends against collateral. A bitcoin base cannot expand; a bitcoin-standard central bank could lend only the coins it held. The 2022 crypto lender failures — Celsius, Voyager, BlockFi, Genesis, FTX — showed what a bitcoin credit system does without a backstop: it recreates 1907 (file 12). A dollarised country like El Salvador already lacks a lender of last resort, which lowered its cost of trying bitcoin and raised the cost of any run into or out of it.
 
**Payment infrastructure.** Legal tender without wallets, liquidity and merchant tools produces El Salvador's outcome: half the population downloaded the app for the bonus and nine in ten never used it again. The Lightning Network's 4,900 coins of public capacity would not cover a small country's daily commerce; the state wallet Chivo was privatised under IMF conditions.
 
**A population willing to use it.** This cannot be legislated. Salvadorans, offered bitcoin with a subsidy and a mandate, kept using dollars; Bhutanese merchants, given a crypto payment system, had no customers. Adoption in the countries that actually use bitcoin most (Nigeria, Argentina, Türkiye) is voluntary and mostly in stablecoins.
 
## The political requirements
 
**A fiscal settlement.** A government that adopts a money it cannot issue gives up seigniorage, the inflation tax and the ability to finance deficits by money creation. The Minneapolis Fed's 2024 paper shows that a state running permanent primary deficits has a modelled incentive to tax or prohibit a fixed-supply competitor. Every major economy runs such deficits — the US federal debt passed $39 trillion in early 2026 — so a bitcoin standard requires either balanced budgets or borrowing real bitcoin from savers at market rates. No major government has offered to do either.
 
**Consensus that survives an election.** Every reserve attempted so far has run through a legislature or a referendum: the Swiss initiative failed, the Polish and Brazilian bills were rejected by their central banks, the US reserve rests on an executive order with no statute. A standard that can be undone by the next government is not a standard; it is a position.
 
## What is still missing, in order of difficulty
 
Ranked from the least to the most tractable: the tax and accounting rules can be changed by statute and partly have been; the AML and Basel rules can be carved out by a determined regulator; the payment infrastructure can be built, and Lightning and custodial wallets are being built; the legal architecture can be written, as El Salvador showed, in a dollarised economy without a monetary union. What cannot be legislated is price stability, an elastic credit system without a lender of last resort, a population that chooses to use it, and a government willing to give up deficit finance. These four are the reasons no country has bitcoin as legal tender in 2026, and they are economic and political, not technical.
 
## Key takeaways
 
Legal tender means creditors and the tax office must accept an asset; it does not make people use it. To adopt bitcoin a country must amend its monetary law, escape any monetary-union treaty, rewrite its reserve statute, carve bitcoin out of Basel's 1,250% risk weight, change its tax base from fiat to bitcoin, build payment infrastructure, and survive IMF conditions — El Salvador managed the first three because it had no currency of its own, and still reversed under the IMF. What remains missing cannot be legislated: volatility an order of magnitude lower than today's, a credit system that does not need a lender of last resort, a population that chooses bitcoin over the dollar, and a government prepared to forgo financing deficits with money it controls. The gap is economic and political, not technical.
