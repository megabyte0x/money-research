# 02 — What Bitcoin Solved in Production, and What It Did Not
 
## The claim, tested against use
 
The whitepaper claimed to solve one technical problem — double-spending without a trusted third party. The community around it claims Bitcoin solves several monetary ones: inflation, confiscation, censorship, slow and expensive settlement, and the opacity of money supply. Seventeen years of production use is enough evidence to sort these claims into three groups: solved and demonstrated in the real world, solved in principle but not in practice, and not solved at all. The comparison throughout is with fiat money as it actually operates — bank deposits, card networks, correspondent banking, central-bank reserves — not with an idealised version of either side.
 
## Solved and demonstrated
 
**Final settlement without an intermediary.** A bitcoin transaction confirmed for an hour cannot be reversed by anyone, at any hour, on any day, between any two points on earth. A SWIFT wire settles in one to several business days through a chain of correspondent banks, each of which can delay, question or refuse it; ACH takes one to three days; a card payment remains reversible for the chargeback window. Bitcoin's base layer has been available 99.99% of the time since January 2009 — a better uptime record than Fedwire, Visa or any bank. This is real and it is used: Ukraine raised over $54 million in crypto donations in the first week of the 2022 invasion and, across government and volunteer wallets, over $212 million during the year, most of it arriving in minutes while its banking system was under attack.
 
**Auditable supply.** Anyone can compute the exact number of bitcoins in existence from the block height, and anyone can verify that no block has ever paid its miner more than the schedule allows. No fiat money offers this. US M2 rose about $7.7 trillion between 2020 and 2026; the decision to create it was taken by a committee, and its size was known to the public after the fact. Bitcoin's issuance for the year 2140 is known today. Whether this is the *right* monetary policy is the subject of file 07; that it is a *transparent* one is not in dispute.
 
**Censorship resistance of self-custodied funds.** This has been tested in at least four episodes. In October 2020 Nigeria's banks froze the accounts of the Feminist Coalition, which was funding the EndSARS protests; it switched to bitcoin and withdrew about $51,000 through it. In February 2022 the Canadian government invoked the Emergencies Act, froze bank accounts of trucker-protest donors without court orders and listed 34 crypto wallets; but 14.6 bitcoins (about $630,000) had already been handed to protesters in person before the injunction, and police recovered 0.28 of them. Russian and Ukrainian civilians moved savings across a closed border in 2022 on hardware wallets. Afghan and Lebanese depositors, cut off by the Taliban and by Lebanon's bank freezes respectively, used it as an exit, though no reliable figures exist. The pattern in every case is identical: funds held on an exchange froze like a bank account; funds held on a key did not. Bitcoin's censorship resistance is a property of self-custody, not of the asset.
 
**Cross-border transfer at near-zero network cost.** The World Bank's global average cost of sending $200 was 6.36% in late 2025 (banks 15%, Sub-Saharan Africa 8.5%); the on-chain cost of a bitcoin transaction in September 2026 was about thirty cents. The qualification, discussed below, is that the network fee is a small part of the cost of a remittance.
 
**A bearer asset that crosses borders as information.** Twelve memorised words are a complete claim on any amount of bitcoin. No other store of value can be carried through an airport, a checkpoint or a bank collapse in that form. Gold in a vault can be frozen (Venezuela's at the Bank of England since 2018; Russia's reserves in 2022); gold in a suitcase can be found. This is the property that makes bitcoin attractive to individuals in failing states and — for the same reason — to sanctioned ones (file 04).
 
## Solved in principle, not in practice
 
**Cheap remittances.** The network fee is trivial but the remittance is not: a Salvadoran in Los Angeles must buy bitcoin with dollars (spread and fee), send it (cheap), and the recipient must sell it for dollars locally (spread, fee, and the volatility between). El Salvador's central bank recorded crypto-wallet remittances at 1.9% of the total in the first months after legal tender, 1.3% in 2023, about 1% in 2024 and 0.7% in the first half of 2026 — $35 million of $5.06 billion. Where the rails are actually used for remittances at scale (Latin America, Sub-Saharan Africa, South and Southeast Asia), the unit sent is overwhelmingly the dollar stablecoin, not bitcoin.
 
**Inflation protection.** Over any period of five years or more bitcoin has outrun every fiat currency — a 25% compound annual return over the five years to end-2025, 70% over ten. But a hedge is something that holds value *when you need it*, and bitcoin fell 50% in two days in March 2020, 64% in 2022 as inflation peaked and the Federal Reserve tightened, and 53% from October 2025 to June 2026 during the Iran war and the oil shock. Argentines, Turks, Nigerians and Lebanese fleeing their currencies overwhelmingly chose the stablecoin dollar, not bitcoin; Chainalysis found over half of Argentina's exchange purchases were stablecoins. Bitcoin protects against *debasement over a decade*; it does not protect against *a bad year*, and a bad year is when protection is needed.
 
**Financial inclusion.** The claim was that the unbanked could hold and send money with a phone. El Salvador is the test: about half of households downloaded the state wallet to collect a $30 bonus, over 60% never transacted again, and users skewed young, male, educated and already banked. Bhutan's tourism payment system, launched with a thousand merchants in 2024, was found in a 2026 field study to have essentially no customers. The unbanked in practice adopted M-Pesa, UPI and Pix — state or telecom rails in local currency — not bitcoin.
 
**Payments.** The whitepaper's "electronic cash" is the function bitcoin performs least. The US Federal Reserve's household survey for 2025 found 10% of adults had held or used crypto in the year, 7% as an investment and under 2% for a payment or remittance. The Lightning Network, built to make bitcoin payments instant and cheap, carried about $1.2 billion a month in late 2025 — roughly 0.15% of stablecoin volume — with public capacity of about 4,900 bitcoins in May 2026, below its 2024 level, and with most consumer usage through custodial apps. In March 2026 Tether put its dollar token on Lightning. Even bitcoin's payment layer is becoming a dollar rail.
 
## Not solved
 
**Price stability.** Bitcoin's realised volatility ran 35% in 2024 and 23–45% in 2025–26; gold's is 9–15%, the major currency pairs' 5–10%. A currency whose purchasing power moves 5% in a day cannot denominate a wage, a rent or a loan; nothing of consequence is priced in it, including — after its legal-tender years — anything in El Salvador. This is the defect from which the others follow (file 11).
 
**Reversibility and consumer protection.** Finality is a feature for a settlement system and a defect for a payment system. There are no chargebacks, no fraud reversal and no recourse against a mistyped address. The FBI recorded $11.4 billion of crypto-related fraud losses in 2025, more than half of all reported internet-crime losses. Fiat's reversibility exists because it is what consumers wanted.
 
**Credit.** Fiat banking's defining function is the creation of elastic credit — loans that create deposits, supported by a lender of last resort. Bitcoin's base is fixed; credit on it exists only as collateralised lending (Coinbase, Xapo, Ledn) that liquidates automatically at a fixed loan-to-value, which is the opposite of elastic. The 2022 attempts to build bitcoin banks (Celsius, BlockFi, Voyager, Genesis) recreated 1907 without a Federal Reserve (file 12).
 
**Custody.** Removing the intermediary transfers the whole burden of security, backup and inheritance to the holder. Between 2.3 and 4 million coins — 11–18% of the total — are estimated lost; Fidelity's analysis found coins ageing into ten-year dormancy at 566 a day, faster than the 450 a day being mined. In 2025 there were 72 verified physical attacks on holders, and in France in early 2026 one kidnapping or home invasion every two and a half days. Exchanges that hold keys for people have lost them at scale: Mt. Gox (850,000 coins, 2014), Bitfinex (119,756, 2016), FTX (an $8 billion customer shortfall, 2022), Bybit ($1.5 billion, 2025). Most people, offered the choice, have chosen the custodian.
 
**Scale.** Three to seven transactions per second on the base layer against Visa's average of roughly 10,000 and a stated capacity of 65,000. Fees are near zero when the chain is quiet and unusable when it is not: the average fee reached $128 on the day of the April 2024 halving.
 
## The honest comparison
 
Set side by side, bitcoin and fiat solve different problems for different people. Fiat is optimised for the daily economy of a functioning state: reversible, stable over a year, elastic in a panic, insured, and useless to anyone the state decides to exclude. Bitcoin is optimised for the case fiat is worst at: a holder who does not trust the issuer, the bank or the border, who is willing to bear volatility and to secure their own keys, and who needs finality more than recourse. In production its demonstrated function is as a store of value and a settlement asset for that holder — "digital gold" with a wire attached — and not as the cash the whitepaper described. That is still a great deal: it is the first bearer asset in history that can be carried as information and verified by anyone, and the first money whose supply is a public fact rather than a policy. It is also much less than a currency.
 
## Key takeaways
 
Bitcoin has delivered, in the real world, four things fiat cannot: final settlement without an intermediary, a supply anyone can audit, funds that cannot be frozen so long as the holder keeps the key, and a store of value that crosses borders as information. It has not delivered cheap remittances, financial inclusion or everyday payments — in each of those the winner has been the dollar stablecoin or a state payment rail — and it has not delivered price stability, reversibility, credit or safe custody, which are the properties ordinary people actually want from money. Its proven role is as a savings and settlement asset for people who distrust institutions, not as electronic cash; the rest of this volume follows from that.
