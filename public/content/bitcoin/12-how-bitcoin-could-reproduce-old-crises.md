# 12 — How Bitcoin Could Reproduce the Old Crises
 
## The thesis
 
The gold era and the fiat era each produced a characteristic set of crises, and after each one the system invented a remedy: clearing houses and the lender of last resort after 1907, deposit insurance after 1933, elastic money after the deflations, money-market reform after 2008, Basel capital rules after every banking collapse. Bitcoin was designed to abolish the institutions that caused those crises — banks, central banks, custodians — and the structure that grew around it (file 01) rebuilt every one of them without the remedies. The result is that bitcoin's short history already contains a bank run, a fractional-reserve collapse, a leveraged-trust bubble, a money-market panic and a margin cascade, each a faithful reproduction of a crisis the older monetary systems learned from. This file matches each part of the bitcoin system to the crisis it can reproduce, with the historical analogue and the numbers.
 
## 1. Custodians: the bank run without a central bank (analogue: 1907, 1930–33)
 
The exchanges and lenders that hold bitcoin for others are banks in everything but law: they take deposits, promise redemption on demand, and — repeatedly — lend or lose the assets. Mt. Gox held 70% of world trading in 2013 and had lost about 850,000 coins by the time it stopped withdrawals in February 2014; the price fell 36% in two months. QuadrigaCX (2019) owed C$216 million to 115,000 customers when its founder died with the only keys; Ontario's regulator called it "an old-fashioned fraud wrapped in modern technology." In 2022 the crypto lenders went in sequence, exactly as the New York trust companies did in October 1907: Celsius froze withdrawals on 13 June 2022 with $4.7 billion of customer liabilities and a $1.2 billion hole, having "lent the same assets over and over"; Voyager, owed $665 million by a single hedge fund, followed within weeks; BlockFi and Genesis fell with FTX, which stopped withdrawals on 8 November 2022 and turned out to have lent more than half its customers' money to its own trading affiliate, leaving an $8 billion shortfall. The Knickerbocker Trust lost $8 million in three hours in 1907 and nine institutions failed in two days because trust companies held thin reserves and sat outside the clearing house with no lender of last resort; the Federal Reserve was the remedy. The 2022 lenders had no reserve requirement, no clearing house, no deposit insurance and no lender of last resort, and the run spread from lender to lender in the same way. "Proof of reserves," the industry's answer, is a snapshot of assets that omits liabilities and cannot detect borrowed coins; the accountant that provided it for the largest exchanges withdrew in December 2022, and the largest exchange pleaded guilty to compliance crimes and paid $4.3 billion in 2023.
 
The channel remains open. Coinbase holds over 80% of ETF coins and about $300 billion of client assets, and lost $360 million in the second quarter of 2026; the question of whether custodial assets are bankruptcy-remote in a stress has not been tested for an entity of that size. A failure there would be Mt. Gox with pension funds as the depositors.
 
## 2. Leverage: the margin cascade (analogue: 1929 margin loans, 1987 portfolio insurance, LTCM 1998)
 
Bitcoin trades around the clock on venues offering 100-times leverage in perpetual futures, with no circuit breakers and no central clearing. On 10–11 October 2025, after a tariff escalation, about $19 billion of futures positions were liquidated in a day — the largest deleveraging in the market's history — and the price reversed from its all-time high; implied volatility jumped from the high thirties to nearly fifty. Automatic, price-triggered selling into a falling market is the mechanism of 1929's margin calls and 1987's portfolio insurance; the difference is that crypto liquidations run continuously and globally with no exchange able to halt them. The convergence-trade version was Three Arrows Capital, which borrowed unsecured to hold 39 million units of the Grayscale trust on the assumption that its premium would persist; when the premium became a 34% discount in 2022 the fund lost over $4 billion and left $3.5 billion of claims across 154 creditors — LTCM's trade with worse counterparties. Open interest and leverage remain the transmission mechanism for every sharp move in 2026.
 
## 3. Treasury companies: the leveraged investment trust (analogue: the 1929 trusts)
 
Strategy is a closed-end fund with one asset and a leverage stack, and it spawned about a hundred imitators in 2025. Its model: sell shares at a premium to the value of its bitcoin, buy bitcoin, raise bitcoin per share, and repeat, with the premium justified by the ability to repeat. It financed 845,000 coins ($63.7 billion) with $25 billion of equity issuance in 2025 alone (the largest issuer in the US market that year), convertible notes, and four series of perpetual preferred stock paying 8–12% — about $1.26 billion a year of fixed dividends against software revenue of about $124 million a quarter. When the premium collapsed — from four times net asset value in late 2024 to below one in November 2025 and about one through 2026, as the shares fell from $417 to $82 — the loop reversed: each share sale now dilutes, dividends must be paid from coin sales, and in June 2026 the company sold bitcoin for the first time since 2022 and raised its preferred coupon to 12%. Coin Metrics estimates it would need to sell about 20,000 coins a year, 2% of its holdings, at $63,000 if capital markets closed. Among the imitators, Semler was absorbed, GameStop and Trump Media exited with losses, and Japan's Metaplanet reported a $725 million quarterly loss on markdowns. The Goldman Sachs Trading Corporation of 1929 — a leveraged trust holding other trusts, valued at a premium justified only by its ability to issue more — fell from $326 a share to under $2 when the premium and the leverage unwound together. No bitcoin treasury company has yet been forced to liquidate; the structure that would force it exists.
 
## 4. Stablecoins: the money-market run (analogue: Reserve Primary Fund 2008, wildcat banks 1837–62)
 
The stablecoin is a par-value claim redeemable on demand against a portfolio, with no deposit insurance — the structure of a money-market fund, and of a nineteenth-century banknote. The runs have already happened. Terra's algorithmic dollar collapsed in May 2022, destroying $40–60 billion in a week; its 19.45% deposit rate was the funding that made the run inevitable. USDC traded at $0.87 on 11 March 2023 when $3.3 billion of its reserves were trapped at Silicon Valley Bank, triggering 3,400 automatic liquidations in decentralised lending, and recovered only because the FDIC guaranteed SVB's depositors — the state backstop the design was meant not to need. Tether traded at $0.88 in October 2018 and $0.945 in May 2022, when $7 billion was redeemed in five days; the CFTC found it fully backed on only 28% of days in a 2016–18 sample, and its first full audit came only in 2026. The Reserve Primary Fund broke the buck on 16 September 2008 with 1.2% of assets in Lehman paper, faced redemptions of half its $60 billion within two days, and was stopped only by a Treasury guarantee. The GENIUS Act requires one-for-one backing in cash and Treasury bills and provides no insurance; Harvard's Kenneth Rogoff has compared the regime to the free-banking era. The BIS has quantified the new channel: stablecoins bought about $35 billion of Treasury bills in 2025, a $3.5 billion flow moves three-month bill yields 2.5–5 basis points, and outflows move them two to three times as much as inflows. A run on a $300 billion stablecoin sector is a forced sale of Treasury bills into a stressed market — the 2008 money-market panic with the crypto system as the trigger and the bill market as the victim.
 
## 5. Deflation: the 1930s in a fixed-supply economy (analogue: 1929–33)
 
An economy denominated in bitcoin cannot expand its money in a downturn; nominal debts rise in real terms as the unit appreciates, wages resist cuts, and there is no central bank to lend. That is Fisher's debt-deflation of 1930–33, and it is the reason the Minneapolis Fed's economists recommend taxing or prohibiting a fixed-supply money rather than adopting it. No economy has been denominated in bitcoin, so the crisis has not occurred; its reserve-asset version has. El Salvador's holdings were down 50% in 2022; Bhutan sold most of a reserve worth a third or more of GDP at the top; Strategy's shares fell 60% in a year; Trump Media abandoned its treasury after $190 million of losses. A state holding a large share of reserves in bitcoin faces a mark-to-market shock in exactly the crisis in which it would need to sell.
 
## 6. Contagion into the banking system (analogue: 2008 shadow banking)
 
The 2023 US bank failures were partly a crypto event. Silvergate's deposits fell from $12 billion to $3.8 billion in the last quarter of 2022 as crypto clients fled after FTX, forcing losses on securities sales and a March 2023 liquidation; Signature had 30% crypto deposits, 89% of them uninsured, lost $10–16 billion of deposits on 10 March 2023 and was closed two days later. Basel's 1,250% risk weight and 1–2% exposure cap, in force from 2025, are the guardrail — and in 2025–26 the OCC opened bank custody and chartered eight crypto trust banks, the Labor Department withdrew its warning on crypto in retirement plans, the ETFs entered 401(k) menus, and bitcoin-backed consumer loans (Coinbase up to $1 million through Morpho; Xapo; Ledn) proliferated, each a pre-programmed liquidation at a fixed loan-to-value. Bitcoin's correlation with equities spikes in stress (30% down in four days in March 2020; a leveraged Nasdaq in 2022; 0.74 with the S&P in March 2026), so the channel opens exactly when the rest of the system is weakest.
 
## 7. Sovereign and concentration risk (analogue: central-bank gold sales; the 1960s gold pool)
 
A government holding 1–2% of supply is a market actor. The US Strategic Bitcoin Reserve's coins "shall not be sold" by an order that the next president can revoke; the Lummis bill would make a single political actor the largest holder on earth. Germany's sale of 50,000 seized coins in June–July 2024 coincided with a fall from $63,000 to $54,000; the Mt. Gox estate's 142,000-coin distribution has hung over the market since 2024, with a final deadline of 31 October 2026. A state that buys can front-run its own announcements; a state that sells can crash the market; a state that holds through a family with mining, stablecoin and treasury businesses has a conflict of interest in every decision.
 
## 8. Mining and security shocks (analogue: the 1970s oil shock, for an economy dependent on one input)
 
China's June 2021 ban roughly halved the hashrate and produced the largest difficulty drop on record; the 2022 bear market bankrupted the largest US miner, Core Scientific. In 2026 the shock is economic rather than political: hashrate has fallen 12–33% from its peak because AI pays more for the same electricity, and the difficulty adjustment lags any shock by up to two weeks, during which blocks slow and fees spike. Three pools produce a majority of blocks and one chip maker supplies 82% of hardware; a state that seized or coerced two pool operators would control block construction for half the network without buying a machine. The security budget's dependence on a subsidy that halves in 2028 (file 11) makes this a growing rather than a receding risk.
 
## 9. Technical black swans (no analogue: the cap is a software promise)
 
On 15 August 2010 a bug created 184 billion coins in one block; in September 2018 another would have let a miner inflate the supply. Both were caught. The quantum migration is the next instance, and the first with a deadline: a third of supply in exposed-key addresses, a machine estimated for 2030–33, and remedies that require a contested consensus change to freeze unmoved coins. A crisis here has no precedent in monetary history because no previous money's supply depended on the correctness of a codebase.
 
## The pattern
 
Each crisis above arises not from bitcoin's protocol but from the structures built on it to make it usable — custodians, lenders, leverage, treasury vehicles, stablecoins, sovereign hoards — and each is a crisis the older systems already had and remedied with institutions bitcoin's design rejects: the lender of last resort, deposit insurance, clearing houses, circuit breakers, elastic money. The bitcoin system has reproduced 1907, 1929, 1930–33, 1998, 2008 and 2023 in miniature within fifteen years. Whether it reproduces them at scale depends on how large the wrappers grow, and in 2026 they are growing into pensions, banks and Treasury bills.
 
## Key takeaways
 
Bitcoin's design removed the institutions that caused the old crises, and the market rebuilt them without the remedies: custodians without a lender of last resort (Mt. Gox 2014, the 2022 lenders, FTX — a rerun of 1907), 100-times leverage without circuit breakers (the $19 billion liquidation of October 2025 — a rerun of 1929 and 1987), leveraged treasury companies valued at a premium they can no longer sustain (Strategy's $1.26 billion of fixed dividends and first coin sales — the 1929 investment trust), stablecoins as uninsured money-market funds that now move the Treasury bill market (Terra, the USDC depeg — the Reserve Primary Fund), and sovereign hoards that can crash the market by selling. The one crisis with no precedent is technical: the 21 million cap is a promise kept by code, and the quantum migration is the first deadline it has ever faced. The channels into the traditional system — ETFs in pensions, bank custody, crypto-backed loans, stablecoins in Treasury bills — widened in 2025–26 while the guardrails were being loosened.
