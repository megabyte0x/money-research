# 05 — Who Favours a Bitcoin Standard, Who Opposes It, and Why
 
## The shape of the argument
 
The world's monetary authorities are not evenly divided on bitcoin. Almost every central bank of a major economy opposes holding it, and every international financial institution — the IMF, the BIS, the Financial Stability Board, the Basel Committee — has written its opposition into policy. The support comes from one government (the United States since 2025, through its executive branch rather than its central bank), one or two small central banks, a handful of small or sanctioned states, and the asset-management industry. The reasoning on each side is worth setting out in full, because it explains both why adoption has taken the form it has (file 04) and what would have to change for it to go further (file 10).
 
## The opponents
 
**The European Central Bank** has produced the most detailed official case against bitcoin, in a series of papers by Ulrich Bindseil and Jürgen Schaaf. "Bitcoin's last stand" (November 2022) argued that bitcoin transactions are "cumbersome, slow and expensive," that it "has never been used to any significant extent for legal real-world transactions," that it "does not generate cash flow or dividends," so its valuation "is based purely on speculation," that its energy use is comparable to Austria's and its electronic waste to the Netherlands', and that the price recovery of late 2022 was "an artificially induced last gasp before the road to irrelevance." Their February 2024 paper on the ETF approvals repeated that "the fair value of an asset without cash flow is zero," cited $24 billion of illicit flows in 2022, and warned of "a renewed boom-bust cycle." Their October 2024 paper made a distinct point: because bitcoin "does not increase the productive potential of the economy," gains to early holders "come at the expense of consumption of the rest of society," so a rising bitcoin "impoverishes both non-holders and latecomers." Christine Lagarde, asked in January 2025 about the Czech proposal, said she was "confident that Bitcoins will not enter the reserves of any of the central banks of the General Council," because reserves "have to be liquid, secure and safe."
 
**The Bundesbank.** Joachim Nagel called bitcoin "digital tulips" in January 2025, said it lacked the security, liquidity and transparency required of a reserve asset, and worried that a government "seal of approval" would mislead the public; in 2026 Germany was weighing him as Lagarde's successor. The Bank of England's Andrew Bailey has said since 2021 that bitcoin has "no intrinsic value" and that holders should "be prepared to lose all your money"; the Bank's 2025–26 stablecoin rules cap individual holdings at £20,000 to protect bank deposits. The Swiss National Bank's Martin Schlegel rejected the reserve initiative in 2025 on three grounds: crypto is "extremely volatile," reserves "need to be highly liquid so that they can be used quickly for monetary policy purposes," and "they are essentially software, and we all know that software can have bugs." Poland's Adam Glapiński: "not under any circumstances, as reserves must be absolutely secure." The Bank of Korea told parliament in March 2025 that bitcoin fails the IMF's reserve criteria on liquidity, market and credit risk. Japan's government said in December 2024 that "the current legal framework does not recognise Bitcoin as part of foreign exchange reserves," whose purpose is to stabilise the yen and bond markets. Australia's Michele Bullock: "It's not a currency, it's not money."
 
**Asia's large economies.** The People's Bank of China banned banks from crypto in 2013, exchanges and token offerings in 2017, and all trading and mining in September 2021; in November 2025 it restated that virtual currencies "lack legal tender status" and that stablecoins enable money laundering and illegal cross-border transfers. The Reserve Bank of India's Shaktikanta Das said in 2022 that cryptocurrencies "do not have any underlying, not even a tulip" and are "a big threat to macroeconomic and financial stability"; India taxes gains at 30% with a 1% withholding on every transfer and no loss offset, and the RBI has blocked the government's own policy paper while insisting central-bank money must remain "the ultimate settlement asset." Russia's central bank governor called for a total ban in 2022; the Duma's finance chairman said in December 2025 that "cryptocurrencies will never become money in Russia," even as Russian exporters settle billions in it.
 
**The Federal Reserve.** Jerome Powell in December 2024: "We're not allowed to own bitcoin. The Federal Reserve Act says what we can own, and we're not looking for a law change." Kevin Warsh, chair since 2026, had called bitcoin "the new gold" for the young and divested his crypto stakes on confirmation; in July 2026 testimony he said, "We do not want to be in the bailout business, full stop — including crypto." Governor Christopher Waller has called bitcoin "electronic gold" while championing dollar stablecoins as extending the dollar's reach — the Fed's actual position, which is to absorb crypto's plumbing into the dollar system rather than to adopt its asset.
 
**The international institutions.** The IMF's 2023 policy framework, "Elements of Effective Policies for Crypto Assets," makes its first element explicit: "do not grant crypto assets official currency or legal tender status," and "central banks should not hold unbacked crypto assets as part of their official reserve assets." It used lending conditions to enforce this in El Salvador and its Article IV process in the Central African Republic and Pakistan. The BIS's Agustín Carstens declared in February 2023 that "the battle between fiat and crypto assets has been won," because "what sustains fiat money is not the application of novel technologies but the institutional arrangements and social conventions behind it"; the BIS's 2025 Annual Report holds that money must pass tests of singleness, elasticity and integrity, which unbacked crypto fails. The Basel Committee's standard, in force from 2025, assigns bitcoin a 1,250% risk weight — a bank must hold a dollar of capital for every dollar of exposure — and caps exposure at 1–2% of Tier 1 capital, which makes bank intermediation of bitcoin uneconomic. The Minneapolis Federal Reserve's October 2024 working paper argues that a fixed-supply asset with no cash flow destroys a government's ability to run permanent primary deficits and that the remedy is "a legal prohibition against bitcoin" or a tax on it.
 
**Outright bans** remain in Algeria, Bangladesh, Egypt, Nepal, Afghanistan and China; Bolivia lifted its ban in 2024, Nigeria reversed its 2021 bank ban in December 2023, and Morocco is drafting a regulatory law.
 
## The reasoning, sorted
 
The objections fall into ten categories, and each is held by a different mix of institutions.
 
*Volatility* (SNB, Bank of Korea, Poland, Japan, Russia's finance ministry, Lagarde): an asset that fell 53% between October 2025 and June 2026 cannot be a reserve whose purpose is to be sold in a crisis. *No intrinsic value* (ECB, Bailey, Das, Nagel): no cash flow, so no anchor for valuation and no floor. *Energy and waste* (ECB, IMF on Pakistan). *Illicit finance* (ECB, PBoC, BIS, FATF, Jamie Dimon): ransomware, sanctions evasion, North Korea. *Financial-stability spillover* (RBI, Bank of England, Basel, FSB): crypto failures reaching banks, as Silvergate and Signature did in 2023. *Loss of monetary sovereignty* (IMF, PBoC, RBI, Nagel): a state that adopts a money it cannot issue loses seigniorage, the lender of last resort and the ability to set rates — the "cryptoization" the IMF warned of in 2021. *Consumer protection* (Bailey, Nigeria's 2021 ban, IMF). *Distribution* (Bindseil and Schaaf): a rising bitcoin transfers wealth from late buyers to early ones without creating anything. *Technical risk* (Schlegel, the US audit that found reserve wallets in desk drawers): bugs, key loss, irreversibility. *Reserve fitness* (Lagarde, Nagel, SNB, Bank of Korea, Japan): the IMF's reserve guidelines require liquidity in a crisis and a legal definition of eligible assets, and bitcoin fails both.
 
The deepest objection, and the one least often stated plainly, is the Minneapolis Fed's: modern states run permanent deficits financed by money whose supply they control, and a credible alternative money with fixed supply is a threat to that arrangement. The gold standard ended for the same reason (file 08).
 
## The supporters
 
**The United States executive.** Donald Trump told the Bitcoin conference in Nashville on 27 July 2024: "Never sell your Bitcoin," promised that government coins would be "the core of the strategic national Bitcoin stockpile," and argued "if we don't embrace crypto and Bitcoin technology, China will." The executive order of 6 March 2025 called bitcoin "a unique store of value" that "permanently caps the total supply at 21 million coins and has never been hacked," and ordered that reserve coins "shall not be sold." The stated reasoning is strategic (deny the asset to rivals, lead the industry), fiscal (a hedge against the national debt — Senator Lummis's BITCOIN Act would buy a million coins to be held twenty years; VanEck modelled that a million coins compounding at 25% a year could offset 18% of a projected $116 trillion debt by 2049, a hypothetical it did not call a forecast), and political (the industry spent heavily in the 2024 election). The limits are equally explicit: Treasury Secretary Bessent ruled out purchases, no statute authorises the reserve, and the Federal Reserve is not involved.
 
**Small central banks and states.** Aleš Michl of the Czech National Bank is the only central-bank governor to argue for bitcoin in reserves — "studying bitcoin won't harm us; on the contrary, it will strengthen us," citing low correlation with equities, bonds and gold, and telling a 2026 conference that a 1% allocation would raise expected return at similar risk. Nayib Bukele of El Salvador remains the most committed head of state. Pakistan's Bilal bin Saqib framed a reserve as "rebirth as a hub of digital innovation." Kazakhstan's central bank plans a staged $300 million from gold and foreign-exchange reserves, "once the dust settles." Bhutan treated mining as an energy export. Argentina's Javier Milei is a vocal admirer who has proposed no reserve and was damaged by promoting a memecoin that collapsed in February 2025. Brazil's and Poland's proposals came from legislators and candidates and were rejected by their central banks; Hong Kong's from individual lawmakers; Germany's from a single member of the Bundestag.
 
**The financial industry.** Larry Fink of BlackRock, whose ETF holds 772,000 coins, told Davos in January 2025 that sovereign funds were discussing 2–5% allocations and that "if you're frightened of the debasement of your currency, you can have bitcoin"; Fidelity's research arm frames it as "a store of value investment, not a currency." Michael Saylor of Strategy calls it "the apex property of the human race." Paul Tudor Jones called it "the fastest horse" against inflation in 2020. Their reasoning is portfolio reasoning — scarcity, low correlation, an inflation hedge — and it aims at bitcoin as a *reserve asset priced in dollars*, not at a bitcoin standard. On the other side of the industry, Jamie Dimon calls it "a pet rock" and "a hyped-up fraud," Warren Buffett called it "rat poison squared," and Nouriel Roubini and Peter Schiff maintain it is worthless.
 
## What divides them
 
The two camps are, in large part, answering different questions. The opponents are asked whether bitcoin can be *money* — a unit of account, an elastic medium, a reserve that can be sold in a panic — and answer, with reasons that the evidence in files 02–04 supports, that it cannot. The supporters, with the exception of El Salvador and the more doctrinaire advocates, are asked whether bitcoin is a *good asset to hold* against currency debasement, and answer that a fixed-supply, uncorrelated, unconfiscatable asset probably is, in small size. The Czech analysis captures the overlap: 1% helps, 5% doubles the risk. Where the two camps genuinely collide is on the question the Minneapolis Fed posed — whether a government that runs deficits should tolerate, let alone hold, a money it cannot print.
 
## Key takeaways
 
The ECB, Bundesbank, Bank of England, SNB, Bank of Japan, People's Bank of China, Reserve Bank of India, Bank of Korea, the Federal Reserve, the IMF, the BIS and the Basel Committee all oppose bitcoin as a reserve or a standard, on grounds of volatility, absence of cash flow, energy, illicit use, financial stability, loss of monetary sovereignty, distribution and technical risk — and the Basel rules make it uneconomic for banks to hold. The support comes from the US executive branch (a seized-coin reserve it will not add to), the Czech central bank ($1 million), El Salvador, Pakistan, Kazakhstan, Bhutan and the asset-management industry, whose case is for bitcoin as a small, dollar-priced hedge against debasement rather than as money. The one genuine collision is fiscal: a state that runs permanent deficits has, as the Minneapolis Fed put it, a reason to tax or ban a money it cannot issue.
