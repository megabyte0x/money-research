# 10 — The Path for Large Economies: How the United States, China, Europe, Japan, India and the Rest Would Get From Here to a Bitcoin Reserve — or a Bitcoin Standard
 
## The ladder
 
Adoption by a large economy, if it happens, will not be a single act but a sequence of steps, each of which is observable and several of which have already been taken. The ladder, in the order every country so far has climbed it, runs: legal clarity (is it legal to hold and trade?); tax treatment (is it property, currency, or something else?); regulated access (futures, ETFs, bank custody); a treasury or sovereign-fund position (state money in a dollar-priced vehicle); a central-bank reserve position (statute change, IMF criteria); settlement use (bitcoin used to clear specific trades between states or firms); invoicing (goods priced in it); and, last and furthest, unit of account. The United States is on the fourth rung with a foot on the fifth; the European Union is on the third and has said it will go no further; China is off the ladder by choice while its neighbours climb; Japan and India are on the second. No country is above the fifth.
 
## The United States: the leader, by executive order
 
The United States is the only large economy whose government has said it wants to lead in bitcoin, and its path is the clearest to trace because most of it is already written into orders and bills.
 
What has been done: regulated futures since 2017; spot ETFs since January 2024 (1.25 million coins, $97 billion); fair-value accounting for companies from 2025; bank custody permitted by the OCC in 2025 and eight crypto trust banks chartered in 2025–26; the Strategic Bitcoin Reserve of forfeited coins by the March 2025 executive order; the GENIUS Act of July 2025 licensing dollar stablecoins; the August 2025 order opening 401(k) plans, with a Labor Department rule in March 2026; state reserves in Texas, New Hampshire and Arizona; and the CLARITY market-structure bill, passed by the House in 2025 and facing a Senate cloture vote on 15 September 2026, which would settle the SEC–CFTC boundary.
 
What would come next: a statute for the reserve (the Lummis bill's million coins over five years has stalled; the May 2026 House successor drops the purchase target, adds a twenty-year lock-up and orders a study of "budget-neutral" funding — gold revaluation, tariff revenue, forfeitures); a de minimis tax exemption so small payments are not capital-gains events; and, in the most ambitious version, Treasury purchases financed by revaluing the gold certificates on the Federal Reserve's books from $42 an ounce to market. The last would be the first purchase of bitcoin with public money by a large state.
 
What stands in the way: the Treasury secretary has ruled out purchases; the Federal Reserve cannot hold bitcoin under its Act and its chair says it will not seek to; a panel of academic economists found zero support for a borrowed reserve; the reserve's only legal basis is an order that the next president can revoke; and the conflicts of interest of the president's family (file 09) make a purchase programme politically radioactive. The US path to a *standard* is, on any realistic view, closed: the dollar's role as the world's unit of account, the $39 trillion federal debt financed in it, and the Minneapolis Fed's demonstration that a fixed-supply competitor threatens deficit finance all mean that the US strategy is to *hold* bitcoin and to *dollarise* its plumbing through stablecoins, not to adopt it as money. The Federal Reserve's Christopher Waller said the quiet part: bitcoin is "electronic gold," and stablecoins extend the dollar's reach.
 
## China: off the ladder, holding the hardware
 
China banned bank involvement in 2013, exchanges in 2017 and all trading and mining in September 2021, and restated the ban in November 2025. It nonetheless matters more to bitcoin than any country except the United States: Bitmain, which makes 82% of the world's mining chips and owns the second-largest pool, is Chinese; an estimated 12% of hashrate still operates in China illegally; the state seized 190,000 coins from PlusToken (and appears to have sold most of them); and Hong Kong, under Chinese sovereignty, has had spot ETFs since April 2024 and is building a dollar- and Hong-Kong-dollar stablecoin regime. China's path is a digital yuan and a cross-border settlement platform that bypasses SWIFT (mBridge), not bitcoin; its strategic interest in bitcoin is as a competitor's asset and a source of hardware exports. A Chinese reserve would require reversing a decade of policy and is not under discussion. Trump's Nashville argument — "if we don't embrace it, China will" — has no counterpart in Beijing.
 
## The European Union: regulated, and closed to reserves
 
The EU built the world's most complete crypto regulatory regime (MiCA, fully in force by mid-2026), which is the third rung of the ladder, and has said at the highest level that it will not climb further: Lagarde's January 2025 statement that no General Council central bank will hold bitcoin, the ECB's papers calling bitcoin's fair value zero, the Bundesbank's "digital tulips," the Basel rules applied in full. The Czech National Bank's $1 million test is the exception that shows the rule — outside the euro, outside official reserves, and rebuked by Frankfurt. The EU's path is the digital euro (pilot 2027, issuance 2029) and gold, which the ECB itself reports overtook the euro as a reserve asset in 2025. A European bitcoin reserve would require a treaty-level change of mind, and a leading candidate to be the next ECB president is bitcoin's most consistent central-bank critic.
 
## Japan, Korea, India: tax first
 
Japan rejected a reserve in December 2024 but in July 2026 passed the largest tax change of any big economy: crypto gains move from a progressive rate of up to 55% to a flat 20% from 2028, with loss carry-forward, which opens the door to domestic ETFs and makes Japan's listed treasury company, Metaplanet (43,000 coins), a template. Korea's central bank ruled out a reserve in March 2025 on IMF reserve criteria; its retail market is among the world's largest. India ranks first in the world in grassroots adoption and has the most hostile tax regime of any large economy — 30% on gains, 1% withheld on every transfer, no loss offset — and a central bank that has blocked the government's own policy paper while pushing its digital rupee. India's path, if any, runs through tax reform and a licensing regime; a reserve is not discussed.
 
## The rest: Russia, Brazil, the Gulf, Switzerland
 
Russia's path is settlement, not reserves: laws in 2024 and 2026 licensed exchanges and crypto for foreign trade while banning domestic payments, its central bank opposes holding it, and its exporters use a rouble stablecoin that moved $93 billion in a year. Brazil has a bill for a 5% reserve that its central bank opposes. The Gulf funds hold ETFs as investments and the UAE mines; Saudi Arabia is in mBridge. Switzerland's population declined to force its central bank into bitcoin. Pakistan, Kazakhstan and Bhutan (file 04) are climbing the ladder faster than any large economy, from a much lower base and, in Pakistan's case, under IMF supervision.
 
## What would push a large economy up the ladder
 
Three triggers appear in every serious scenario. The first is a sanctions or reserve-freezing event that persuades a state its dollar assets are not safe — the trigger that sent central banks to gold after 2022. But gold is the beneficiary of that trigger precisely because it is not dependent on American infrastructure, and bitcoin, with 37% of its hashrate, its ETFs, its custodians and its pricing stablecoin under US jurisdiction, is not the obvious alternative for a state at odds with Washington. The second is a fiscal or inflationary crisis in a major fiat currency severe enough to run Thiers' Law — people fleeing the currency into anything scarce. Bitcoin's 2026 record suggests it would be sold first in such a crisis and bought afterwards; the initial flight would be into dollars and gold. The third is competitive adoption: if the United States were to buy a million coins, other states would fear being left holding nothing while a rival's reserve appreciated — the game-theoretic argument that Larry Fink and the Bitcoin Policy Institute make. This is the only trigger that starts with a large economy rather than with a crisis, and it depends on a US purchase that the US has so far declined to make.
 
## A realistic sequence
 
If large-economy adoption proceeds, the record so far suggests it would look like this. First, more states hold bitcoin as an investment through funds and ETFs (Abu Dhabi, Norway indirectly, Texas), booked outside reserves. Second, one or two mid-sized central banks follow the Czech model into 1% allocations with statutory cover, and the IMF's reserve guidelines are tested. Third, the United States converts its forfeited coins into a statutory reserve and, in a favourable Congress, funds small purchases from gold revaluation or tariffs — the first public purchase by a large state, and the trigger for competitive holdings by Japan, the Gulf and Brazil. Fourth, bitcoin becomes collateral in regulated credit (bank custody, bitcoin-backed Treasury products) and a settlement asset in specific corridors. Fifth — and this is the step no large economy has approached — a country in fiscal or monetary crisis lets prices and taxes migrate, which has only ever happened toward the dollar.
 
Each step widens bitcoin's role as a reserve and collateral asset priced in dollars; none of them makes it the unit of account of a large economy. The path for large economies, on the evidence of 2026, leads to bitcoin as a small line in the reserve portfolio beside a much larger line of gold — and to the dollar, through stablecoins, as the currency in which that line is measured.
 
## Key takeaways
 
Adoption climbs a ladder — legality, tax, regulated access, treasury position, central-bank reserve, settlement, invoicing, unit of account — and no large economy is above the fifth rung. The United States, the only large economy that wants to lead, has a seized-coin reserve by executive order, ETFs, bank custody, a stablecoin law and state reserves, but no statute, no purchases, a central bank that cannot hold bitcoin, and a fiscal reason never to adopt it as money; its strategy is to hold the asset and dollarise its plumbing. China has banned it while supplying 82% of its hardware; the EU regulated it and closed the door to reserves; Japan cut its tax to 20%; India taxes it into the ground. The triggers that could move a large economy — a reserve freeze, a fiat crisis, a competitive US purchase — favour gold in the first two cases and depend, in the third, on a purchase Washington has declined to make. The realistic path ends with bitcoin as a small reserve line beside gold, measured in dollars.
