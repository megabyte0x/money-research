# 11 — The Defects That Have Stopped Bitcoin From Becoming a Global Currency
 
## Sorting the defects
 
Bitcoin's critics list dozens of faults and its defenders answer most of them. The useful exercise is to separate the defects that are *fixable* (by software, by markets or by law) from those that are *structural* (consequences of the design that cannot be removed without removing what makes bitcoin bitcoin), and to note which of them are getting better or worse. The ten below are ordered roughly from the most to the least tractable. The first four have partial remedies under way; the last six are the reasons bitcoin is a reserve asset and not a currency.
 
## 1. Legal and regulatory perimeter — being fixed
 
For a decade bitcoin's status was undefined: property in the United States, banned in China and India's banks, unregulated in Europe. That is ending. MiCA has governed the EU since 2024–25; the GENIUS Act licensed dollar stablecoins in July 2025; the OCC opened bank custody in 2025; Japan reclassified crypto as a financial asset in 2026; the FATF's Travel Rule reaches 99 jurisdictions. What remains is the US market-structure bill and the 1,250% Basel risk weight that keeps bitcoin out of bank balance sheets. The perimeter is now a set of rules rather than a void — but the rules place bitcoin inside the dollar system's supervision, which is a fix for the defect and a defeat for the design.
 
## 2. Tax and accounting — partly fixed
 
Fair-value accounting arrived in the US in 2025 and made corporate holdings possible. The tax problem remains: bitcoin is property in the US, so every purchase is a capital-gains event "regardless of the amount," with no exemption for small transactions; India's 30% rate and 1% withholding are designed to suppress use. So long as tax is assessed in fiat, fiat is the legal unit of account. A de minimis exemption is proposed and unenacted.
 
## 3. Scalability — mitigated, not solved
 
The base layer clears three to seven transactions a second, against Visa's average of about 10,000 and stated capacity of 65,000; block space is fixed at one megabyte (four million weight units). The fee market is bimodal: near zero when the chain is quiet (about thirty cents in 2026) and unusable when it is not — an average of $128 per transaction on the day of the April 2024 halving, when a token craze filled the blocks. The scaling answer, the Lightning Network, has gone sideways: public capacity peaked above 5,500 coins in 2024, fell to about 3,850 in August 2025, recovered to 5,600 in December and settled at 4,900 ($380 million) by May 2026; channels are half their 2023 peak; the top ten operators hold 62% of capacity; "inbound liquidity remains the number-one operational challenge"; most consumers use it through custodial apps; and its fastest-growing payload since March 2026 is Tether's dollar. Volume grew — 12 million transactions in late 2025 — but the network was designed for a billion. Scaling has been solved for the users who accept a custodian, which is to say by reintroducing the bank.
 
## 4. Custody, irreversibility and physical risk — structural, with mitigations
 
No chargebacks, no recourse, no reset. Between 2.3 and 4 million coins are lost. Exchanges holding keys for others have lost 850,000 coins (Mt. Gox), 120,000 (Bitfinex), $8 billion (FTX) and $1.5 billion in one 2025 attack (Bybit). The FBI recorded $11.4 billion of crypto-related fraud in 2025, more than half of all reported internet-crime losses, with victims over sixty losing $4.4 billion. Physical coercion has become an industry: 72 verified "wrench attacks" in 2025, and in France in 2026 one kidnapping or home invasion every two and a half days, with $124 million taken worldwide in the first half of the year. Multi-signature wallets, hardware devices and regulated custodians mitigate this — and every mitigation is a step back toward the trusted third party the design removed. Most holders have chosen the custodian; 6% of supply sits with one.
 
## 5. Concentration — structural and worsening
 
File 09 sets out the numbers: three mining pools produce a majority of blocks, six template producers over 95%, one chip maker 82% of hardware; five people merge the code, one donor funds most development; one custodian holds over 80% of ETF coins; one company holds 4% of supply on leverage; the top 2% of entities hold about 70%. Decentralisation of the *rules* is intact. Decentralisation of everything else has declined every year since 2020, and the ETF, which brought the asset to pensions, is the main cause.
 
## 6. Energy and the security budget — structural and worsening
 
Bitcoin's security is paid for by the block subsidy, which halves every four years by design. In 2026 fees supplied 0.4–0.7% of miner income — a ten-year low — so the network's roughly $30 million a day of security spending is 99% subsidy. In 2028 the subsidy halves again; by the 2040s it is negligible. Either fees rise to tens of millions of dollars a day, which the fee market has never sustained and which would price out ordinary use, or security falls, or the community adopts a perpetual "tail emission" (Peter Todd's proposal) and abandons the 21 million cap that is the asset's defining promise. Eric Budish's work at Chicago shows why this matters: proof-of-work is secure only while the continuous cost of mining exceeds the one-time gain from an attack, which makes it "a very expensive form of trust" whose cost cannot fall as the asset grows. Meanwhile the industry that provides the security is leaving: with production costs around $78,000–80,000 a coin against a 2026 price often below that, listed miners signed over $70 billion of AI-hosting contracts, sold 32,000 coins in the first quarter, and unplugged about a fifth of their machines; hashrate fell 12–33% from its 2025 peak. Bitcoin's electricity use — about 138 terawatt-hours a year, half a percent of the world's, 52% from sustainable sources — is the visible cost; the invisible one is that a higher-margin buyer of the same megawatts has appeared.
 
## 7. Governance and technical risk — structural
 
The 21 million cap is a property of software written by a few people. On 15 August 2010 an integer-overflow bug created 184 billion coins in one block; it was reversed within hours by a soft fork. In September 2018 an inflation bug that would have let a miner spend the same output twice was patched before disclosure. Both were caught; both show the cap is a promise kept by maintenance, not a law of nature. The community's mechanism for decisions was demonstrated in the 2017 block-size war (nodes beat miners and companies) and again in the 2025–26 dispute over data in transactions (a fifth of nodes defected to a rival client with one maintainer). It works, slowly, for disputes with no deadline. The quantum question has a deadline. Google's March 2026 paper cut the estimated qubits needed to break bitcoin's signatures to under half a million; NIST finalised post-quantum standards in 2024 and the US government requires migration by 2035; industry estimates put a cryptographically relevant machine at 2030–33. Between 1.7 million coins (the oldest exposed-key outputs, including Satoshi's) and 6.9 million (a third of supply, counting every revealed public key) are vulnerable. The proposals — a new address type (BIP-360) and a phased freeze of unmigrated coins (BIP-361) — require the community to agree to confiscate, by deadline, the holdings of anyone who does not move, including a founder who has not moved since 2010. Nothing in bitcoin's history suggests it can make that decision quickly, and the ossification that makes the cap credible is exactly what makes the decision hard.
 
## 8. Unit-of-account failure — structural
 
Nothing of consequence is priced in bitcoin: not wages, rents, mortgages, bonds, invoices or taxes, not in El Salvador during its legal-tender years, not on its own Lightning Network, where the growing payload is dollars. Every bitcoin payment is a fiat transaction with an exchange-rate step in the middle. This follows from volatility and from tax law, and it means bitcoin cannot perform the third function of money without first performing the first two at a stability it has never shown.
 
## 9. Volatility — structural
 
Realised volatility of 35% in 2024, 23% in the calmest year on record (2025), 23–45% through 2026; against 8–9% for gold and the S&P 500 in 2024 and 5–10% for major currencies. Drawdowns of 94% (2011), 86% (2013–15), 84% (2018), 78% (2022) and 53% (2025–26). Monthly moves of −10% and −15% in the first two months of 2026. Fidelity's defence that bitcoin is now no more volatile than Netflix concedes the point: a currency should not trade like a growth stock. Lawrence White's explanation is that the volatility is designed in — gold's supply responds to price and bitcoin's cannot, so every change in demand goes into price — and there is no evidence of convergence toward gold's level; the ratio of bitcoin's to gold's volatility fell below two in late 2025 for the first time and rose again in 2026.
 
## 10. The macro objections — structural
 
No elasticity, no lender of last resort, no seigniorage, and a supply that cannot respond to a panic: file 07 sets out why this is the objection that ended the gold standard and why it applies to bitcoin with more force. The IMF's "cryptoization" concern is the emerging-market version — crypto lets residents dollarise, circumvents capital controls and accelerates outflows in a crisis. The Minneapolis Fed's version is the fiscal one: a fixed-supply asset with no cash flow threatens permanent deficit finance, and a rational government would tax or ban it. And the deepest version is the dependency: bitcoin is priced in dollars. Stablecoins are 40% of all crypto trading and Tether's dollar carries 70–80% of bitcoin pairs; Tether holds $115–140 billion of Treasuries; the reference price is set on offshore venues in dollars. Bitcoin's liquidity is a derivative of dollar liquidity, which is the opposite of what a reserve currency requires and is why bitcoin is sold in the first hours of every crisis in which dollars are scarce.
 
## The pattern
 
The fixable defects — legal status, tax, accounting, custody products, scaling for custodial users — are being fixed, and every fix has the same shape: it reintroduces an intermediary (a custodian, an ETF, a bank, a regulator, a stablecoin issuer) and moves bitcoin further inside the dollar system it was built to escape. The structural defects — volatility, inelasticity, the fee-and-subsidy cliff, concentration, governance without a deadline, dollar dependency — are consequences of the fixed supply and the leaderless design, and cannot be removed without removing bitcoin's reason to exist. That is why bitcoin has become a reserve asset held through dollar wrappers rather than a currency: the market fixed what it could and priced the rest.
 
## Key takeaways
 
Bitcoin's tractable defects — undefined legal status, tax and accounting treatment, custody, and scaling for people who accept a custodian — are being solved, and each solution reintroduces an intermediary and puts bitcoin deeper inside dollar supervision. Its structural defects are not solvable: volatility of 23–45% a year that Lawrence White shows is designed into a vertical supply curve; a security budget that is 99% subsidy, halves in 2028, and is losing its miners to AI; concentration of custody, mining, hardware and development that worsens each year; a governance process that cannot enforce a deadline against a quantum threat to a third of supply; a unit-of-account role it has never filled; and a price, liquidity and settlement system denominated in the dollar it was built to replace. Those are the reasons it is a reserve asset and not a global currency.
