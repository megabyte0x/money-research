# 07 — If Bitcoin Were the Unit of the Economy: The Supply System, Fixed Versus Variable Rates, Backing, and How Denomination Would Shift
 
## Three things people mean by "a Bitcoin standard"
 
The phrase covers three different arrangements, and most disagreement about it comes from people arguing about different ones. The first is bitcoin as *base money and unit of account* — wages, prices, debts and taxes denominated in it, and every other form of money either bitcoin or a claim on it. This is the true analogue of the classical gold standard, and it is what the question in this file's title asks about. The second is bitcoin as a *reserve asset* held by treasuries or central banks alongside gold and Treasuries while the dollar (or the euro, or the rupee) stays the unit of account; this is what the US executive order, the Lummis bill and every asset manager actually propose. The third is bitcoin as a *privately held monetary good* that competes with gold as savings and never becomes a currency; this is Fidelity's and BlackRock's framing and, in practice, the world of 2026. Only the first raises the hard questions of fixed supply, exchange-rate regime and backing. Almost all real adoption so far is the second and third.
 
## The supply system: what a fixed base does to an economy
 
Bitcoin's supply schedule is the whole of its monetary policy. About 20.08 million coins exist in September 2026; 450 a day are added (0.8% a year), which halves to 225 a day in April 2028 and to nothing meaningful after the 2040s; the ceiling is 21 million, and an estimated 2.3–4 million of those are already lost. Netting the lost coins against coins ageing into permanent dormancy, the effective supply is roughly 16–17 million and is, by one Fidelity analysis, already shrinking.
 
If such an asset were the unit of account of a growing economy, the price level would have to fall at roughly the rate of real growth. With output rising 2–3% a year and money fixed, the only way for the quantity equation to balance is deflation of 2–3% a year, rising toward the world growth rate once new issuance stops. Warren Weber's 2016 Bank of Canada paper — the only rigorous model of a bitcoin standard by a central-bank economist — reaches exactly this: "mild deflation and constant exchange rates." Interest rates would be set by the market for loanable funds with no policy rate; nominal rates would sit below real rates by the rate of deflation; and government deficits could be financed only by taxing bitcoin or borrowing real bitcoin from savers at whatever rate they demanded. There would be no monetary financing, no quantitative easing, no lender of last resort beyond whatever bitcoin the state held.
 
The case that this is benign rests on George Selgin's distinction between deflation caused by falling costs (harmless, even desirable, since prices should fall when goods get cheaper to make) and deflation caused by collapsing demand (destructive). The pro side's empirical exhibit is the "Great Deflation" of 1873–96, when prices in Britain and America fell 1–2% a year under gold while output and industry grew strongly. Saifedean Ammous's *The Bitcoin Standard* (2018) adds that hard money lowers society's time preference and points to the innovation of the 1871–1914 gold era; Lyn Alden's *Broken Money* (2023) argues that fiat's discretionary supply creates a structural bias toward debt. Notably, Alden — the most careful of the advocates — describes bitcoin's present role as "a store of value and global settlement layer rather than a medium of exchange or unit of account," and even a sympathetic reviewer of Ammous notes that he does not address the deflation objection at all.
 
The case against is the standard macroeconomics of the last ninety years. Irving Fisher's debt-deflation theory (1933): when prices fall, the real burden of every fixed nominal debt rises, forcing distress sales that push prices down further — and under a bitcoin standard every mortgage, bond and wage contract would be written in an asset designed to appreciate, a structural bias toward creditors. Sticky wages: employers cannot cut nominal pay 2% a year without conflict, so deflation shows up as unemployment. Barry Eichengreen's *Golden Fetters* documents how the interwar gold standard transmitted deflation across the world and how mass democracies, unlike the propertied electorates of 1880, would not accept the wage cuts the rules demanded; the countries that left gold recovered first. Lawrence White, an economist sympathetic to gold and to bitcoin, identifies the decisive difference between them: when gold's purchasing power rises, mining expands and supply responds; "the supply curve for bitcoin is always vertical," so every shift in demand goes entirely into the price. That is why bitcoin is more volatile than gold and would remain so as money.
 
The word for what a fixed base lacks is *elasticity*. A panic is a sudden rise in the demand to hold money; under the US national banking system of 1873, 1893 and 1907 the base could not expand to meet it and the result was bank runs, and the Federal Reserve Act of 1913 was written "to furnish an elastic currency." Under a bitcoin standard the only way to recreate elasticity is fractional-reserve banking in bitcoin — banks issuing bitcoin deposits beyond their bitcoin reserves — which recreates credit and, with it, runs; Weber's model is explicit that "crises would not be eliminated." The alternative is full-reserve banking, in which every deposit is backed one-for-one (which is what Lightning channels and custodial bitcoin already are), which removes runs and also removes credit: the money supply is exactly 21 million and a panic resolves itself entirely through the collapse of every other price.
 
## Fixed rate or variable rate? The five regimes
 
A country adopting bitcoin as its monetary anchor can choose one of five arrangements, each with a historical precedent.
 
| Regime | Exchange-rate result | Who bears the volatility | Monetary policy | Precedent |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| (a) Full bitcoinisation — bitcoin *is* the currency | No domestic exchange rate; the economy floats against the dollar with bitcoin | Every household and firm; creditors gain in deflation | None; no lender of last resort unless the state holds bitcoin | El Salvador 2021–25; dollarisation (Ecuador 2000, Zimbabwe 2009) |
| (b) National currency defined as a fixed weight of bitcoin | Fixed at par among all bitcoin-defined currencies, since arbitrage is nearly free | Domestic prices and wages adjust (Hume's mechanism) | None; no independent rates | Classical gold standard 1880–1913 |
| (c) Fiat fractionally backed by bitcoin reserves | Fixed until the peg is doubted | The issuing centre; the periphery through reserve runs | Partial, until confidence breaks | Gold-exchange standard 1925–31; Bretton Woods 1944–71 |
| (d) Bitcoin as a floating reserve asset beside gold | Existing float unchanged | The central bank's balance sheet, marked to market | Fully retained | What states actually do in 2025–26 |
| (e) 100%-backed "bitcoin dollars" (currency board) | Fixed one-to-one to bitcoin; a bitcoin stablecoin | Holders, in dollar terms | None | Currency boards; wrapped and custodial bitcoin |
 
**Fixed rate: regime (b), the classical standard.** If each country defined its currency as a weight of bitcoin — say 1,000 rupees to a millibitcoin — then every such currency would trade at par with every other, because moving bitcoin between them costs almost nothing. This is the fixed-rate world of 1880–1913. Adjustment works as David Hume described for gold: a country that imports more than it exports loses bitcoin, its money supply shrinks, its prices fall until its goods are cheap enough to restore balance. No country can set its own interest rate. Weber predicts what would follow: central bankers "would face pressure to return to a fiat system so that a more activist monetary policy could be pursued" — the same political economy that ended gold in 1931. A fixed-rate bitcoin standard is technically simple and politically the least survivable option.
 
**Backing fiat: regime (c), the exchange standard.** Could a currency be "backed" by bitcoin the way the dollar was backed by gold under Bretton Woods, with a central bank promising convertibility at a fixed rate from a fractional reserve? Yes, and history shows exactly how it ends. To supply the world with reserves the centre must issue more claims than it holds bitcoin — the Triffin dilemma — and when claims visibly exceed the reserve, holders run (France on the dollar in 1965–71). Fractional bitcoin backing also reintroduces the discretionary money creation the standard was meant to abolish; whatever reserve ratio is chosen is the ratio the market will one day test. A currency "backed" by bitcoin is a promise by a central bank, and its value rests on the same trust as fiat, plus the volatility of the backing.
 
**Variable rate: regime (d), the floating reserve.** This is the only regime any state has adopted or is considering. The dollar, euro or rupee remains the unit of account; the central bank or treasury holds some bitcoin as it holds gold; the exchange rate floats as before; monetary policy is untouched. The price of admission is mark-to-market exposure: El Salvador's reserve was down 50% in 2022 and up 130% in 2025; the Czech central bank's own study found a 5% allocation doubled the volatility of its reserves. The proposals in this class — the Lummis bill's million coins held twenty years as a debt hedge; VanEck's model of a million coins compounding at 25% a year to offset 18% of US debt by 2049 (a hypothetical, its authors stress); the Bitcoin Policy Institute's "BitBonds," Treasuries with 10% of proceeds in bitcoin and shared upside — all add a volatile asset to the sovereign balance sheet and none changes the unit of account.
 
**Regime (e), the bitcoin stablecoin.** A token fully backed by bitcoin is simply bitcoin with a custodian; it inherits all of bitcoin's dollar volatility and provides no elasticity. Its precedent is a product, not a monetary regime.
 
The answer to the question, then, is that a bitcoin standard "works" at a fixed rate only in the sense that the classical gold standard worked — with fixed exchange rates, no monetary policy, adjustment through domestic deflation, and eventual abandonment when the deflation became politically intolerable — and that it "works" at a variable rate only by not being a standard at all, but a reserve asset. Backing fiat with a fractional bitcoin reserve recreates Bretton Woods and its collapse.
 
## How would everything come to be denominated in bitcoin?
 
Money acquires its functions in a sequence: it becomes a collectible, then a store of value, then a medium of exchange, then a unit of account. Vijay Boyapati's "Bullish Case for Bitcoin" and Ammous both use this Mengerian ladder and place bitcoin between the first two rungs; the medium-of-exchange stage begins, in their account, when purchasing power stabilises, and the unit-of-account stage when goods are priced in the asset itself, not converted from fiat at the moment of sale. For bitcoin to reach the top rung, four things would have to be true that are not true now.
 
First, volatility would have to fall to something like gold's or a major currency's — under 10% a year against the goods people buy. Bitcoin's calmest year was 23%; in 2026 it moved 25% in two months. Prices cannot be sticky in a unit that is not. Second, wages and debts would have to be written in bitcoin. No large employer pays in it, no lender issues mortgages in it, no sovereign borrows in it; even the bitcoin treasury companies borrow in dollars. Third, taxes would have to be assessed in bitcoin. Every state, El Salvador included, computes tax in fiat, and a fiat tax base makes fiat the unit of account by law: each bitcoin payment is a fiat sale with a capital gain. Fourth, a price layer in satoshis — a consumer price index, a wage index, a yield curve — would have to exist. It does not; sats solve divisibility and nothing else.
 
Two laws of monetary economics govern the transition. Gresham's Law — bad money drives out good — operates when a legal-tender rule fixes the rate between two monies: people spend the overvalued one and hoard the undervalued one. Thiers' Law — good money drives out bad — operates when people are free to choose and the bad money is failing fast, as in Zimbabwe in 2008 or Germany in 1923. El Salvador is a clean demonstration of the first: with a mandatory-acceptance law and a stable dollar, bitcoin was hoarded and never priced anything. The second would run in bitcoin's favour only if fiat were collapsing — which is why every "hyperbitcoinisation" scenario is, on inspection, a fiat-hyperinflation scenario. Daniel Krawisz's 2014 essay that coined the term describes "a voluntary transition from an inferior currency to a superior one" that happens "much faster than you expect"; the critics' reply is that it presupposes the collapse it claims to cause.
 
The history of dollarisation shows what the last step looks like. In Latin America in the 1980s, Ecuador in 2000 and Zimbabwe in 2009, the unit of account switched *after* the store of value and the medium of exchange had already switched, only when the domestic currency's expected loss was large and persistent — and the winner every time was the deepest, most stable, most widely held currency, the dollar, never the scarcest. Gita Gopinath's dominant-currency research shows why the dollar's position reinforces itself: most trade is invoiced in it, most foreign debt is denominated in it, hedging markets are built around it, and reserves are held in it; a 1% rise in the dollar predicts a 0.6% fall in trade between third countries. The IMF found in 2021 that crypto adoption in emerging markets "reinforces dollarisation forces," and the evidence bears it out: in Argentina, Türkiye, Nigeria and Lebanon the crypto rails carried mostly dollar stablecoins.
 
Which produces the central paradox of this file. The instrument that lets people use bitcoin's rails — the stablecoin — is 99% dollars, is backed by US Treasuries, carries 70–80% of bitcoin trading, and since March 2026 runs on bitcoin's own payment layer. The GENIUS Act licensed it. A Federal Reserve note in December 2025 concluded that dollar stablecoins "could reinforce dollar internationalisation," and an IMF paper found that stablecoin flows rise when the dollar strengthens, "helping satisfy global dollar demand." The plumbing of the bitcoin economy is a dollar economy. Every year that bitcoin's adoption grows through ETFs, stablecoin pairs and dollar-denominated custody, the dollar becomes *more* entrenched as bitcoin's unit of account, not less.
 
The realistic sequence for a shift toward bitcoin denomination, if one occurs, therefore runs through the reserve function rather than around it: bitcoin becomes a reserve asset of states and companies; it becomes collateral in credit markets and a settlement asset between parties who distrust each other's banks; specific trades — energy, mining, cross-border services between sanctioned or capital-controlled economies — begin to be invoiced in it; and only after a prolonged loss of confidence in the fiat unit would domestic prices, wages and taxes follow. Each step is observable, and in 2026 only the first two have begun.
 
## Key takeaways
 
A bitcoin standard in the strict sense — bitcoin as the unit of wages, prices, debts and taxes — would mean a fixed money supply in a growing economy, hence secular deflation of 2–3% a year, market-set interest rates, deficits financed only by taxing or borrowing real bitcoin, and no lender of last resort; the pro case (Selgin, Ammous, Alden) says productivity-driven deflation is benign, the anti case (Fisher, Eichengreen, White, Weber) says debt-deflation, sticky wages and a supply that cannot expand in a panic are exactly what ended the gold standard. At a fixed rate it would reproduce the classical gold standard, with fixed exchange rates, no monetary policy and adjustment by domestic deflation; fractional bitcoin backing of fiat would reproduce Bretton Woods and the Triffin dilemma; at a variable rate it is not a standard but a reserve asset, which is the only version any state has adopted. For prices to be denominated in bitcoin, volatility would have to fall below 10%, wages, debts and taxes would have to be written in it, and Thiers' Law would have to run against fiat — which requires a fiat collapse. Meanwhile the stablecoin, the bridge that makes bitcoin usable, is making the dollar more entrenched as bitcoin's own unit of account.
