# 01 — The Origin: What 2008 Produced, and the Currency Structure That Grew Around It
 
## Where this volume starts
 
The previous two volumes meet at the end of the Bretton Woods dollar–gold promise: the United States suspended official conversion on 15 August 1971, and the [IMF's 1978 reform](https://www.imf.org/external/pubs/ft/pam/pam45/pdf/chap2.pdf) later removed gold from the international par-value framework. That did not erase central-bank gold reserves or all domestic reserve-backed currencies. The subsequent dollar system expanded through offshore banking, trade invoicing, cards, derivatives, exchange-traded funds and stablecoins, alongside other monetary arrangements. Bitcoin was designed for transfers without a central operator. This volume asks what that design solved and how it has actually been used.
 
## The moment of birth
 
Lehman Brothers failed on 15 September 2008. Six weeks later, on 31 October 2008, a nine-page paper titled *Bitcoin: A Peer-to-Peer Electronic Cash System* was posted to a cryptography mailing list under the name Satoshi Nakamoto. On 3 January 2009 the first block was mined; embedded in it was that morning's headline from The Times of London, "Chancellor on brink of second bailout for banks." The first transaction — ten bitcoins from Nakamoto to the cryptographer Hal Finney — sits in block 170 on 12 January 2009. The first published exchange rate, in October 2009, was 1,309 bitcoins to the dollar, derived not from any trade but from the electricity cost of mining one. On 22 May 2010 a Florida programmer paid 10,000 bitcoins for two pizzas, the first recorded purchase of a physical good. Mt. Gox, a Tokyo exchange originally built for trading game cards, opened in July 2010 and by 2013 handled over 70% of all bitcoin trading.
 
The timing is the point. Bitcoin was not designed in a vacuum as an interesting piece of computer science; it was released into the month in which the fiat system revealed that its deepest assumption — that the institutions issuing money and holding deposits were sound — could fail, and that the answer would be to create more money to rescue them. The genesis headline is the inventor's editorial comment. Everything Bitcoin does is a negative image of what the 2008 bailouts did.
 
## What the design actually is
 
Stripped to its parts, Bitcoin is four ideas bolted together, three of them borrowed and one new.
 
The first is a public ledger of every transaction ever made, copied in full by every participant who wants one. There is no master copy; there are, as of 2026, roughly 23,000 reachable copies and many more behind private networks. The second is a fixed issuance schedule written into the software: 50 new coins per block in 2009, halving every 210,000 blocks (roughly every four years) — 25 in November 2012, 12.5 in July 2016, 6.25 in May 2020, 3.125 since 19–20 April 2024 — until issuance rounds to zero around 2140 at a ceiling of 21 million. The third is proof-of-work, borrowed from Adam Back's 1997 Hashcash: to add a block of transactions, a participant must find a number that makes the block's hash fall below a target, which requires enormous trial-and-error computation but is trivial for anyone else to verify. The difficulty of that puzzle adjusts every 2,016 blocks so that, however much computing power joins or leaves, blocks keep arriving about every ten minutes; on 6 September 2026, at block 965,664, the difficulty rose 1.3% to 127.45 trillion.
 
The fourth idea, the new one, is what the whitepaper's title claims: a way to prevent a digital token from being spent twice without anyone in charge. Every earlier attempt at digital cash — David Chaum's DigiCash (founded 1989, bankrupt 1998), e-gold (1996, operators indicted 2007, shut 2009), Liberty Reserve (seized by the US Department of Justice in May 2013), and the design essays of Wei Dai's b-money (1998) and Nick Szabo's bit gold — had either a company that kept the ledger or no working answer to who kept it. A company can be sued, seized, hacked or bankrupted, and every one of them was. Nakamoto's answer was that the longest chain of valid proof-of-work blocks *is* the ledger, that anyone may extend it, and that rewriting history would require out-computing the rest of the world for as long as the rewrite had to be maintained. Trust in an institution was replaced with a cost of attack.
 
The result is a monetary object with a set of properties no previous money had all at once: its supply follows publicly verifiable issuance rules; transfers can be checked without asking a central operator; and self-custody puts spending authority with the key holder rather than an account provider. A transaction becomes harder to replace as blocks accumulate, but no fixed elapsed hour makes it absolutely final: block arrival varies, reorganizations remain possible, and the confirmation depth appropriate to a payment depends on its value and risk. Six confirmations are a common high-value convention, not a guarantee. [Bitcoin Developer Guide, “Verifying Payment”](https://developer.bitcoin.org/devguide/payment_processing.html#verifying-payment). The base layer has limited throughput, which is one reason its whitepaper's “electronic cash” ambition has not translated into universal everyday payment use.
 
## How a currency structure formed around it
 
Bitcoin by itself is a ledger and a token. A currency needs a market, a price, custodians, credit, bridges to the rest of the financial system, and eventually the attention of the state. Each of these formed in sequence, and each of them reintroduced exactly the intermediaries the design was built to remove.
 
The exchanges came first (Mt. Gox, 2010) and gave bitcoin a dollar price — $31 in June 2011, $1,000 in November 2013, $19,783 in December 2017, $69,000 in November 2021, $100,000 on 5 December 2024, and an all-time high of $126,198 on 6 October 2025. Between each peak the price fell 75–94%. Wallets, both self-custodied and hosted, followed. From 2011 a broader asset class of imitators and forks appeared (Litecoin, then thousands of "altcoins"; Ethereum in 2015 added programmable contracts; Bitcoin Cash split off on 1 August 2017 over block size), so that "crypto" became a market of which bitcoin is roughly half.
 
The decisive structural innovation came in 2014 and was not bitcoin at all. Tether issued a token on the Bitcoin network redeemable one-for-one for a dollar. The stablecoin solved the trader's problem — how to hold dollars inside a crypto exchange without a bank — and in doing so made the dollar the unit of account of the entire crypto system. By 2026 dollar stablecoins are a $300-billion-plus market, 99% of it in dollars, carrying about 70–80% of all bitcoin trading pairs and settling more volume than Visa. The Bitcoin economy runs on dollar rails. This paradox — that the money built to escape the dollar is priced, traded, collateralised and, on its own second layer, increasingly *paid* in dollars — recurs in every file of this volume.
 
Then came the regulated bridges. CME listed bitcoin futures in December 2017. Grayscale's trust let stock-market investors hold bitcoin through a security; in August 2020 MicroStrategy, a software company, began converting its treasury into bitcoin and became the model for a "treasury company." On 10 January 2024 the US Securities and Exchange Commission, having refused for six years on grounds of market manipulation and having lost in court to Grayscale, approved eleven spot bitcoin exchange-traded funds. BlackRock's IBIT became the fastest-growing ETF in history; by 11 September 2026 the US spot funds held 1.25 million bitcoins, $96.6 billion, about 6% of everything ever mined, with Coinbase holding custody for over 80% of it. Bitcoin had been financialised in exactly the way gold was by the ETF in 2004 (file 08 of the After Gold set): a thing you had to store became a line in a brokerage account.
 
Finally the state arrived. El Salvador's 2021 Bitcoin Law required acceptance; Decree 199, adopted in January 2025 and effective 90 days after publication, made private acceptance voluntary, removed tax-payment authority and restricted public participation, while retaining “curso legal” wording in the statute. It amended, not repealed, the law. [Decree 199, arts. 1–8](https://www.jurisprudencia.gob.sv/DocumentosBoveda/R/2/2020-2029/2021/06/1080B6.HTML?embedded=true). Elsewhere governments acquired coins through seizures, and the United States created a Strategic Bitcoin Reserve by executive order in March 2025. Dollar stablecoins and institutional custody brought further state rules to services built around the asset; those developments do not themselves make Bitcoin a national unit of account.
 
## What kind of thing this is
 
By September 2026, seventeen years in, the structure that has grown around Bitcoin looks less like a rival currency and more like a new layer of the existing one. At its centre is an asset of fixed supply and no issuer, worth about $1.55 trillion — roughly 5% of the world's above-ground gold and 1.5% of its broad money — held by about 365 million people, three ETF sponsors, one software company with 845,000 coins, and a dozen governments. Around it is a dollar-denominated market: stablecoins for pricing and settlement, futures and ETFs for institutional access, exchanges and custodians for holding, and a regulatory perimeter built by the United States. The thing itself keeps its original properties — no one can inflate it, freeze it in self-custody, or switch it off — and everything built around it has the properties of ordinary finance, including the ability to fail.
 
That is the currency structure Bitcoin created after 2008: not a replacement for fiat but a parallel, hard-capped, bearer asset that the fiat system has spent a decade absorbing on its own terms. The rest of this volume examines what that asset has solved, where it is used, who has adopted it and why, what it would take to make it a unit of account, who controls it, and where it could break.
 
## Key takeaways
 
Bitcoin's design offers a ledger anyone can verify, an auditable issuance schedule and transfers without a central operator. Confirmations make replacement progressively harder; they do not produce absolute finality at a fixed time. [Bitcoin Developer Guide, “Verifying Payment”](https://developer.bitcoin.org/devguide/payment_processing.html#verifying-payment). Around the protocol, exchanges, custodians, lenders and funds reintroduced intermediary risks, while dollar stablecoins became important to crypto-market trading. Bitcoin is a parallel monetary asset, not an observed replacement for the dollar as the unit of account.
