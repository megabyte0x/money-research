# 02 — Before Money: Ornament, Ritual, Power and Other Uses

Before coinage, gold served as ornament, treasure and a weighed metal in some places. Its uses and meanings varied across societies; later monetary acceptance was not automatic.

## Ornament and status

Adornment is one of gold's earliest documented uses. At Varna the gold was beads, bracelets, pectorals, sceptre fittings and, in Grave 43, a penis sheath — items of display on a single high-status male, buried with him. The message was rank. Comparable uses appear in several regions, including the Royal Cemetery of Ur in Sumer (~2600–2500 BCE) with Queen Puabi's gold headdress, the Mycenaean shaft graves with the "Mask of Agamemnon" (~1550 BCE), Egyptian collars and pectorals, Scythian animal-style goldwork, the gold of the Indus cities, the Moche and Chimú of Peru. Jewellery could be portable, visible and inheritable wealth, and it remains a substantial use of gold in some settings. ## Religion and the divine

In Egypt gold was "the flesh of the gods," specifically of the sun-god Ra; it did not tarnish, so it was the material of eternity. Tutankhamun's innermost coffin (~1323 BCE) is about 110 kg of solid gold and his funerary mask about 10 kg. Temples were gilded, statues of gods were made of or plated with gold, and Egyptian pharaohs sent gold as diplomatic gifts to Near Eastern kings, who complained in the Amarna letters that "gold in Egypt is as common as dust." In Mesopotamia and later Israel, sanctuaries and cult objects (the Ark, the Menorah) were of gold. In India gold is associated with Lakshmi and prosperity and is still bought on auspicious days; in Buddhist Southeast Asia, gold leaf is pressed onto stupas and images. The link between gold and the sacred is one reason gold was hoarded by temples, and some temples stored valuables and participated in lending, though their roles differed from modern banks — in Babylon, Greece (Delphi, Delos) and Rome (the Temple of Saturn held the state treasury, the *aerarium*).

## Burial and the afterlife

Because gold does not corrode, it was the natural material for the grave. Varna, Ur, Mycenae, Egypt, the Scythian kurgans, the Anglo-Saxon Sutton Hoo ship (~625 CE), the Sican tombs of Peru — a huge share of the gold we have from antiquity comes out of tombs. Burial removed these objects from use; the total effect on ancient monetary circulation is unknown.

## Tribute, gifts and plunder

Gold moved between rulers as tribute, gifts, ransom and loot in documented societies. Ancient writers describe Croesus' gifts to Delphi and Alexander's seizure of Persian treasuries; their quantities and the later use of particular metal need exact textual locators. Rome's Dacian conquest is associated in later accounts with Trajan's payments and games, but the earlier precise per-citizen sum, duration and gold-causation claim are withheld pending a primary text with passage and context. These cases show possible royal-treasure and payment roles, not a modern official reserve system.

## Practical and technical uses

Gold's malleability gave it uses beyond display. Gilding covered some wood, bronze, stone and manuscripts; gold thread also appears in historical textiles. Etruscan gold dental appliances are among documented early examples, but a worldwide dental first or an unbroken standard-through-the-twentieth-century claim needs archaeological and clinical locators. Some medical and alchemical traditions used or described gold; their dates, purposes and effects vary. Gold's conductivity and corrosion resistance support particular modern electronic and technical applications; the World Gold Council's 2025 technology-demand category needs its table definition rather than an inference that gold appears in every connector. ## A unit of weight before a unit of money

Egyptian and Mesopotamian records used weight units to account for goods and metal; Egyptian *deben* and Mesopotamian *shekel* values depended on the period and metal. The Code of Hammurabi includes silver-denominated obligations. The former precise Egyptian and Mesopotamian weights and gold–silver ratios need specific text, translation, date and unit context before being quoted as facts. In several ancient regions, metals could be transferred or accounted for by weight before stamped coinage became common; an account unit, a weighed payment and a coin are different arrangements.

## Key takeaways

Gold was prized in many societies — for beauty, permanence, sacredness and rank — long before it was spent. It was used for jewellery, temples, tombs, diplomacy, gilding, textiles, dentistry and medicine, and it moved between rulers as tribute and plunder. Coinage certified weight and authority, but acceptance still depended on markets and political institutions.
