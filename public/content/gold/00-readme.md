# Gold: From Bare Metal to World Money and Back — Research Directory

A structured set of research notes answering one long question: how did a yellow metal dug out of riverbeds become the unit that everything else was measured in, how did it share and then lose that job to silver, copper, paper and finally the US dollar, and why does it still hold value today even though nothing is priced in it anymore?

Compiled 7 September 2026. Historical material is drawn from standard numismatic and economic-history sources; present-day figures come from the World Gold Council, the ECB, the IMF and market reports current to early September 2026. Every file ends with a "Key takeaways" section so the whole arc can be skimmed by reading only those.

## The arc in one paragraph

Gold was never chosen by anyone. It is a metal that does not rust, is soft enough to work by hand, is rare enough to matter, and is found lying in rivers as pure nuggets and dust — so it was the first metal humans ever worked, around 4600 BCE at Varna on the Black Sea, long before it was money. For roughly four thousand years it was treasure, ornament, and the stuff of gods and kings, while silver and copper did the actual work of trade by weight. Coins were invented in Lydia around 650–600 BCE from electrum, a natural gold–silver alloy, and King Croesus separated them into pure gold and pure silver pieces around 550 BCE, creating the first bimetallic system. From then until 1971 gold was, in most places and most eras, the top tier of a three-metal ladder: gold for large payments, state treasuries and international settlement; silver for wages, taxes and daily commerce; copper and bronze for small change. Paper money appeared as a claim on those metals. Britain slid onto a pure gold standard by accident after Isaac Newton mispriced the guinea in 1717, and its commercial dominance dragged the rest of the world onto gold in the 1870s. Two world wars and a depression broke that system, and in 1944 the world pegged to the US dollar, which alone stayed convertible into gold. In 1971 the United States ended even that. Gold has had no official monetary role since 1976, yet central banks now hold over 36,000 tonnes of it and were buying at record pace in 2022–2026, and it has overtaken both the euro and US Treasuries as a share of global reserves. It survives as the one monetary asset that is nobody's liability.
