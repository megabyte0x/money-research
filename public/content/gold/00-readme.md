# Gold: From Bare Metal to World Money and Back — Research Directory

A structured set of research notes answering one long question: how did a yellow metal dug out of riverbeds become a monetary standard, how did it share and then lose that job to silver, copper, paper and finally the US dollar, and why does it still hold value even though most contemporary wages, prices and debts are not stated in gold?

Compiled 7 September 2026. Historical material is drawn from standard numismatic and economic-history sources; present-day figures come from the World Gold Council, the ECB, the IMF and market reports current to early September 2026. Every file ends with a "Key takeaways" section so the whole arc can be skimmed by reading only those.

## The arc in one paragraph

Gold's durability, workability and relative scarcity made it useful for ornaments and later for some monetary roles, but social practice and state rules determined whether it was accepted as money. Gold objects from the Varna cemetery (c. 4600–4300 BCE) are among the earliest known worked gold; copper working predates them. Silver by weight, copper, credit and other media also served trade in different regions. Electrum coins appeared in western Anatolia around the seventh century BCE, and Croesus later issued separate gold and silver coins; neither event began a universal three-metal system. Britain's 1717 mint ratio helped gold displace silver in circulation, its 1816 law formalized a gold standard, and several major economies adopted gold-linked arrangements in the late nineteenth century. The classical standard, interwar restorations and Bretton Woods were different systems. Under Bretton Woods the dollar was redeemable in gold for foreign monetary authorities; the US suspended that conversion in 1971. The [IMF's 1978 reform](https://www.imf.org/external/pubs/ft/pam/pam45/pdf/chap2.pdf) removed gold as the common denominator of the international par-value system, without abolishing official gold reserves or every later domestic use. Zimbabwe's [2024 ZiG announcement](https://www.rbz.co.zw/index.php/monetary-policy/monetary-policy-statements/1389-2024-monetary-policy-statement) described a reserve basket containing foreign currency and precious metals, mainly gold; the [IMF later classified its exchange-rate arrangement as de jure floating and de facto other managed](https://www.imf.org/-/media/files/publications/cr/2025/english/1zweea2025001-source-pdf.pdf). That announcement does not establish fixed gold-weight redemption or continuing reserve coverage.
