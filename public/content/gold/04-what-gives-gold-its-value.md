# 04 — What Gives Gold Its Value, and Why Everything Was Denominated in It

## The question properly stated

"What gives gold value?" hides two questions. The first is why gold, rather than some other substance, became the reference point. The second is why a reference point is needed at all — why one good ends up as the unit that all other goods are priced in. Both have answers, and neither is "because it is shiny."

## Why one good becomes money at all

Carl Menger's 1892 essay *On the Origins of Money* gives the classic account. In barter, every trader faces the problem of the double coincidence of wants. Traders learn that some goods are more *saleable* than others — easier to pass on to a third party — and start accepting those goods even when they do not want them, because they can re-trade them. Whatever is most saleable snowballs: the more people accept it, the more saleable it becomes. Money is therefore not invented by decree but emerges as the most marketable commodity, and the state's later role is to certify and standardize it. (An alternative school, associated with anthropologist David Graeber and the chartalists, argues that credit and state-imposed tax obligations came first and coined money later; the archaeological record of Mesopotamian silver-by-weight, which predates coins by two thousand years, actually supports both — silver was a unit of account for debts and taxes long before it was a coin.)

## Why gold is the most saleable good

Aristotle, in the *Politics* and *Ethics*, listed what a good needs to serve as money, and later writers (Jevons, Menger, Mises) refined the list. Gold scores at or near the top on every criterion:

**Durability.** Gold does not corrode, tarnish, rot or decay. Wheat, cattle, salt, cloth, iron and even silver (which tarnishes) all lose value in storage. A gold coin from 550 BCE is still fine gold.

**Scarcity with stability.** Gold is rare enough that a small amount carries large value (portability), but its supply is not erratic. Because almost all gold ever mined still exists, the above-ground stock is about 60 times annual mine output, so even a bumper year of mining raises supply by only 1.5–2%. Copper and iron can be produced almost without limit; cowrie shells or wampum collapse in value when a new source is found (as African cowrie money did when Europeans shipped Maldivian shells in bulk).

**Divisibility and fungibility.** Gold can be split into any weight and every gram is identical to every other. Diamonds are scarce and durable but each one is different and cannot be recombined.

**Verifiability.** Density, colour, the touchstone, the acid test and later fire assay let anyone check gold cheaply. This lowered the cost of every transaction.

**Uselessness, in a sense.** Gold's industrial demand is small (about 7% of annual demand), so its price is not whipsawed by the needs of industry, and hoarding it does not deprive anyone of a tool. Copper's industrial demand made it a poor money for exactly the opposite reason.

Silver shares most of these properties, which is why silver was money for as long as gold, and file 05 explains how the two divided the work. But silver is roughly 15–20 times more abundant in the crust, tarnishes, and — crucially, in the nineteenth century — its supply was expanding faster than gold's just when the world was standardizing.

## Why prices came to be denominated in gold

Once gold is the most widely accepted good, it becomes the natural unit of account because it minimizes the number of prices anyone has to remember. In an economy with N goods and barter, there are N(N−1)/2 exchange ratios; with one money, only N−1. States adopted it because tax collection, army pay and tribute across a large territory needed a unit that was stable, portable and accepted at both ends of the empire. The Persian daric, the Roman aureus and solidus, the Islamic dinar, the Venetian ducat and the British sovereign each became the reference unit for a trading world far larger than the state that issued it, because merchants everywhere knew what they were getting.

Note the historical nuance: for most of history the *everyday* unit of account was silver (the shekel, drachma, denarius, penny, rupee, tael), and gold was the unit for large values and the reserve that anchored the silver. Gold became the sole denominator only in the era of the classical gold standard, roughly 1870–1914.

## Is gold's value "intrinsic" or a social convention?

Both, and the distinction is less sharp than it sounds. Gold's *physical* properties are objective and make it a better candidate than alternatives. But its *price* is a social fact: it is worth what people will give for it, and they give a great deal because they expect everyone else to do the same, indefinitely. This expectation has now held across every civilization for six thousand years, which is a stronger track record than any other asset, institution or state possesses. Economists call this a coordination equilibrium or a Schelling point. The Bitcoin design of 2009 was explicitly an attempt to reproduce gold's scarcity and verifiability in digital form, which is itself evidence of what people think gold's value rests on.

A second way to see it: gold is the only widely held monetary asset that is not somebody's liability. A dollar is a liability of the Federal Reserve; a bond is a liability of its issuer; a bank deposit is a liability of the bank. Gold owes nothing to anyone and cannot default, be printed or be frozen by the issuer. Its value therefore tends to rise when trust in issuers falls — in wars, inflations, sovereign crises — and to fall when real interest rates on the alternatives are high. In that sense gold's price is a running measure of how much the world trusts its governments and currencies.

## What gold's value is *not*

It is not its use value. If gold were priced as an industrial metal it would be far cheaper. It is not its yield: gold pays no interest and costs money to store and insure. It is not government backing: governments have repeatedly tried and failed to hold its price down (the London Gold Pool, 1961–68) or up. It is not rarity alone: rhodium and osmium are rarer. It is the combination of physical suitability and a universal, self-reinforcing, historically unbroken expectation of acceptance.

## Key takeaways

Money emerges as the most saleable good; gold became the most saleable good because it is durable, scarce but steady in supply, divisible, fungible, verifiable and largely useless for anything else. Prices were denominated in it (and in silver) because a single unit collapses the number of prices and lets states tax and pay across distance. Its value today is a coordination equilibrium six thousand years old, reinforced by its being the one monetary asset that is no one's debt.
