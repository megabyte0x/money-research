## The last four years in one paragraph

Gold traded around $1,800 in 2022 when the US and EU froze Russia's dollar reserves. Central banks, especially in Asia, Eastern Europe and the Middle East, responded by buying more than 1,000 tonnes a year for three years, the highest sustained official demand since the 1960s, and the price rose through $2,000, $3,000 (early 2025), $4,000 (late 2025) and briefly to about $5,590 on 28 January 2026 before correcting to the $4,400–4,700 range through summer 2026. The drivers were, in order: reserve diversification away from the dollar after 2022; US fiscal deficits and the erosion of confidence in the long end of the Treasury market; geopolitical fragmentation (tariffs, the Ukraine and Middle East wars); Chinese retail investors moving out of property into gold; and, from 2025, Western investors returning via ETFs. Goldman Sachs' September 2026 forecast is $4,900 by year-end, J.P. Morgan's higher; both cite central-bank buying as "a multi-year trend rather than a trade." Forecasts are forecasts.

## What still gives gold value

**It is the only reserve asset that is no one's liability.** Every other reserve — Treasuries, bunds, gilts, bank deposits — is a promise by a government or bank, which can inflate, default, or be frozen. Gold in a vault in your own country cannot. 2022 turned this from a theoretical point into policy for every central bank outside the Western alliance.

**Its supply is fixed by geology, not policy.** Mine output grows 1–2% a year and is near its geological ceiling (below-ground reserves would last only about fifteen years at current rates, though new discovery keeps replenishing them). Against currencies whose supply expanded by trillions in 2008–2021, that scarcity is the whole argument.

**Six thousand years of unbroken acceptance.** Gold is the Schelling point: everyone expects everyone else to accept it, and that expectation has never failed. No currency, company or state has a comparable record.

**Real physical demand.** About 40% of annual demand is jewellery and technology, mostly in India and China, where gold is the traditional form of household savings; that floor does not depend on financial sentiment.

**Regulation.** Under the Basel III framework (phased in 2019–2022) allocated physical gold held in a bank's own vault carries a zero risk weight for capital purposes, while unallocated paper gold is penalized under the net stable funding rules — a regulatory nudge toward holding the physical metal.

**Inflation and real rates.** Gold tends to rise when real interest rates (bond yields minus inflation) fall or turn negative, because the cost of holding a non-yielding asset falls. Its price is, in effect, a running vote on whether currencies will keep their purchasing power.

## What gold does *not* do today

It is not a unit of account: no wages, prices or debts are set in it. It is not a medium of exchange except in tiny niches. It pays nothing and costs money to store. Its dollar price can fall 30–50% over a few years (1980–85, 2011–15) and stay down for decades (1980–2005). It is a store of value across generations, a hedge against monetary and political failure, a reserve asset for states, and a savings vehicle for households in the Global South — the roles it has played since Egypt, minus the coin in the pocket.

## Why it is still not "the" unit of economy

Because the world chose elasticity over discipline. A monetary system anchored to gold cannot create money in a crisis, cannot support a modern banking system's credit expansion, and cannot pay for wars and welfare without the deflation that bimetallic and gold-standard economies suffered in the 1870s–90s and 1930s. Fiat money delegates the question "how much money should exist?" to central banks instead of to the mining industry; the price of that is chronic low inflation and the occasional bout of high inflation, and gold's rising price is the market's charge for that risk. Gold has therefore become what it was for most of history before 1870: the reserve underneath the system rather than the system itself. The difference from Persia or Byzantium is that no one is legally obliged to convert into it, so its value is now purely the sum of six thousand years of habit and a very good chemistry.

## Key takeaways

At end-2025 market prices, gold's 27% share of the ECB's broad official-reserves measure exceeded the euro's 15% and US Treasuries' 22%; the ECB says this was largely a gold-price valuation effect, not evidence by itself of reserve managers selling dollar assets. At a constant end-2023 gold price, gold and the euro would each be about 16% and Treasuries 26%. The IMF's dollar share of **foreign-exchange** reserves has a different denominator that excludes gold. Gold remains a reserve asset without an issuer's promise, but it is not the unit in which most contemporary wages, prices or debts are stated.
