# 11 — Glossary

**Alluvial / placer gold** — Gold eroded from rock and deposited in river gravel as dust, flakes and nuggets; gathered by panning or washing. An important source in several early assemblages and later gold rushes; exact archaeological provenance varies by site.

**Assay** — A test of a metal's purity. Ancient methods: touchstone streak, density; medieval and modern: fire assay (cupellation), acid test, X-ray fluorescence.

**Aureus** — Roman gold coin, ~8 g, regularized by Julius Caesar; 1 aureus = 25 silver denarii.

**Bezant** — Western European name for the Byzantine gold solidus/nomisma.

**Bimetallism** — A legal monetary arrangement that assigns a fixed mint or payment ratio to gold and silver. The legal ratio can diverge from the market bullion ratio; rules differ by jurisdiction.

**Bretton Woods system (1944–1971)** — Post-war adjustable-par-value arrangement in which the US dollar's official gold-conversion price was $35 per fine ounce for foreign monetary authorities, while other participating currencies commonly used adjustable dollar pegs. [IMF, *Financial Organization and Operations*, sixth ed., ch. III p. 91](https://www.imf.org/external/pubs/ft/pam/pam45/pdf/chap3.pdf); [IMF history, ch. 25](https://www.elibrary.imf.org/display/book/9781451971477/ch025.xml).

**Bullion** — Gold or silver in bulk form (bars, ingots) valued by weight and purity rather than as coin.

**COFER** — IMF's Currency Composition of Official Foreign Exchange Reserves database. Its denominator is foreign-exchange reserves and excludes monetary gold, SDRs and IMF reserve positions; the allocation method changed from 2025 Q3.

**Croeseid** — Separate refined-gold and silver coin issues associated with Croesus of Lydia around the mid-sixth century BCE. Exact first-in-world status, issue dates and equivalences require an issue catalogue.

**Daric** — Achaemenid Persian gold coin associated with Darius I; issue-specific weights and its circulation extent require a numismatic catalogue.

**Debasement** — Reducing the metal weight or fineness of a coin while retaining its denomination. Price effects depend on issue, acceptance and other conditions.

**Deben** — Egyptian weight unit (~91 g) used to price goods in copper, silver or gold.

**Dinar** — Islamic gold coin of 4.25 g, from 696–697 CE; the word derives from the Roman *denarius*.

**Ducat** — Venetian gold coin of 3.5 g, struck unchanged from 1284 to 1797.

**Electrum** — Gold–silver alloy, natural or manufactured. Early western Anatolian coins used electrum; their fineness varied by issue and needs assay evidence.

**ETF (gold)** — Exchange-traded fund whose shares provide exposure to gold under its own holdings, custody and redemption rules. WGC reported 801 t of net additions to global gold ETF holdings in 2025.

**Fiat money** — Money issued without a fixed legal promise to redeem it for a specified commodity weight. Its use depends on law, tax obligations, payments infrastructure and confidence; an issuer can still hold commodity reserves.

**Fineness / carat** — Purity of gold. 24 carat = pure (999+ fine); 22 carat = 916 fine (sovereigns, Indian jewellery); 18 carat = 750.

**Florin** — Florentine gold coin first issued in the mid-thirteenth century; it was an important later Western European gold issue, not the start of all regional gold coinage.

**Gold-exchange standard** — A gold-linked arrangement using claims on a reserve currency with its own official gold relationship. The legal right, holder and redemption chain differed across British India, interwar arrangements and Bretton Woods; the three were not one identical convertibility promise.

**Gold standard (classical)** — Gold-linked arrangements among participating economies, roughly 1871–1914, with fixed gold definitions and relatively stable exchange-rate parities. Coin and note redemption rights, restrictions and implementation varied by jurisdiction.

**Gold window** — The US Treasury's facility for converting foreign official dollars into gold at $35/oz; closed by Nixon on 15 August 1971.

**Good Delivery bar** — LBMA-standard bar of ~400 troy ounces (~12.4 kg), 995+ fine; the form in which central banks and bullion banks hold gold.

**Gresham's law** — In a system that enforces a legal exchange ratio between coin types, a market mismatch can favour spending the legally overvalued type and hoarding, melting or exporting the undervalued type. Actual circulation depends on enforcement, costs and trade conditions.

**Hacksilber** — Cut pieces of silver objects used as money by weight in the Bronze and Iron Age Near East.

**Legal tender** — A legally designated means of discharging specified monetary debts. Its effect, exceptions and scope depend on jurisdiction; it does not mean every seller must accept it before a sale.

**LBMA** — London Bullion Market Association; sets the twice-daily benchmark gold price and bar standards.

**Lode / vein gold** — Gold within quartz veins in rock, requiring mining and crushing.

**Mithqal** — Weight of ~4.25 g used for gold in the Islamic and West African trade; equal to the dinar.

**Mohur** — Mughal gold coin of ~11 g.

**Petrodollar** — Dollar receipts from oil exports, and sometimes the subsequent investment or lending of those receipts. Dollar invoicing predates the 1974 US–Saudi economic-cooperation commission; this is not a dollar-for-oil redemption right or a claim that every oil sale used dollars. [GAO, 1979](https://www.gao.gov/assets/id-79-7.pdf); [Federal Reserve staff memorandum, 1975](https://fraser.stlouisfed.org/title/federal-open-market-committee-meeting-minutes-transcripts-documents-677/meeting-may-20-1975-22476/content/fulltext/FOMC19750502Memo01).

**Price-specie flow mechanism** — David Hume's model of adjustment under specie-linked trade: gold outflows could affect domestic money, prices and competitiveness. Credit, policy and other trade changes can interrupt or alter that sequence; it is a model, not a guaranteed historical outcome.

**Reserve asset / reserve currency** — External assets held by monetary authorities for balance-of-payments, intervention or related reserve purposes; holding gold does not by itself create a gold-redemption promise. A reserve currency is a currency widely held in foreign-exchange reserve portfolios.

**SDR (Special Drawing Right)** — IMF synthetic reserve asset created 1969, valued against a basket of major currencies.

**Seigniorage** — The issuer's profit from creating money: the difference between a coin's face value and its metal cost, or the interest saved by issuing non-interest-bearing currency.

**Shekel** — Mesopotamian weight (~8.3 g) and later coin; the basic silver unit of account of the ancient Near East.

**Solidus** — Constantine's gold coin of 4.5 g (72 to the Roman pound), 309/312 CE; stable for ~700 years.

**Sovereign** — British gold coin of 7.32 g fine gold (7.988 g at 22 carat), defined 1816–17; still struck.

**Stater** — Generic Greek/Lydian term for a standard coin, gold or silver.

**Stock-to-flow** — Ratio of an estimated existing stock to new annual output. The result depends on the stock boundary and the period measured; it does not predict a price.

**Token coin (subsidiary coin)** — Coin whose denomination exceeds its metal value. Issue limits, legal-tender limits and any exchange or redemption rights depend on the issuing law.

**Touchstone** — Fine-grained dark stone on which gold is rubbed; the colour of the streak, compared to reference needles, indicates purity. In use from the Lydian period.

**Triffin dilemma** — Tension identified by Robert Triffin in the Bretton Woods dollar–gold system: growing foreign dollar claims could support international liquidity while straining confidence in official gold conversion. It is an analysis of that arrangement, not a law that every reserve issuer must always run a trade deficit.

**Troy ounce** — 31.1035 g; the unit in which gold is priced.

**Unit of account** — The unit in which prices, wages and debts are expressed. Distinct from medium of exchange (what is handed over) and store of value (what is held).
