# 08 — Why the Dollar, Not Gold, Is the World's Unit of Account

## The short answer

The dollar replaced gold for three reasons that reinforce one another. First, at the moment the post-war system was designed (1944) the United States held most of the world's gold and was the only intact major economy, so the dollar was the only currency credible enough to stand in for gold. Second, once everyone held and traded dollars, the network effect took over: a currency is useful in proportion to how many others use it, and no rival — not gold, not the euro, not the yuan — has matched the dollar's depth, liquidity and legal infrastructure. Third, and most fundamentally, a fiat currency can do things gold cannot: it can expand with the economy, be created in a crisis, earn interest, and be lent, cleared and settled electronically at zero cost. Governments and markets chose those advantages over gold's discipline.

## Step one: the dollar becomes gold's proxy (1944–1971)

Bretton Woods made the dollar the only currency convertible into gold and made every other currency convertible into dollars. That gave central banks a reason to hold dollars instead of gold: dollars (in the form of US Treasury bills) paid interest, gold did not, and dollars could be turned into gold on demand — or so it seemed. By 1971 foreign official dollar holdings were several times the US gold stock. When Nixon closed the gold window, the world's central banks were left holding dollars with no metal behind them — and they kept holding them, because there was nothing else to hold. The dollar had inherited gold's role by default.

## Step two: oil and the petrodollar (1974 onward)

After the 1973 oil shock, the United States and Saudi Arabia agreed that Saudi oil would be priced and sold in dollars and the proceeds recycled into US Treasury securities; OPEC as a whole followed. Since every country needs oil, every country needed dollars, which created a permanent global demand for dollar balances unrelated to trade with the US itself. The same logic spread to most internationally traded commodities, including gold itself, which is priced in dollars per troy ounce.

## Step three: the infrastructure of dollar dominance

**The Treasury market.** US government debt — about $28 trillion outstanding in the mid-2020s, traded around the clock — is the largest, most liquid financial market in the world. A central bank can park $50 billion in Treasuries in an afternoon and get it back the next day. Gold has no equivalent: the whole above-ground stock is worth around $30 trillion at 2026 prices, but only a fraction trades, it earns nothing, and moving it means moving bars.

**Eurodollars and offshore banking.** From the 1960s, banks outside the US (starting in London) took dollar deposits and made dollar loans, creating a global dollar credit system that no other currency has. Most international lending, bond issuance and trade invoicing is in dollars, including trade between two non-US countries.

**Clearing and legal reach.** Dollar payments clear through US banks and the SWIFT/CHIPS systems, giving the US both the plumbing and the power to sanction. This is a strength (everyone is on one system) and, since 2022, a visible weakness (see below).

**Stability and rule of law.** The dollar has never been repudiated, the Federal Reserve is credible on inflation over decades, and US courts enforce contracts. Reserve status rests on trust, and the US has, since 1945, been the most trusted issuer.

## Step four: why gold could not do the job

Gold's supply grows 1.5–2% a year regardless of what the economy needs. Under a gold standard, a growing economy tends toward deflation (falling prices, which raise the real burden of debt), and a financial panic cannot be met by creating money, because money is metal. The nineteenth century's deflations and the Great Depression's severity are the historical bill for that. Modern central banking — lender of last resort, inflation targeting, quantitative easing — is impossible on a gold anchor, and after 1933 no democracy was willing to accept mass unemployment to protect a gold parity.

Gold also does not earn a return. A central bank holding $100 billion in Treasuries earns interest; one holding $100 billion in gold pays storage and insurance. Gold is expensive to verify and transport at scale, cannot be used to settle a payment in milliseconds, and its price in terms of goods is volatile over months even if stable over centuries. And, decisively, no state wants to give up the power to create its own money: fiat currency is seigniorage, war finance and crisis management in one instrument.

Finally, gold is not a unit of account because nothing is priced in it. A unit of account is the thing invoices, wages, contracts and debts are written in. That is the dollar (and the euro, yen, pound, yuan and every national currency for domestic use). Gold is priced in dollars, not the reverse; it is a store of value, occasionally a medium of settlement between central banks, but not a measuring stick.

## What replaced gold's function, precisely

Gold under the classical standard did three things: it was the unit everything was priced in, the reserve behind every currency, and the means of settling international balances. After 1971 the dollar took all three. Countries hold dollars (Treasuries) as reserves, price trade in dollars, and settle in dollars. The "backing" of the dollar is not metal but the US economy's capacity to tax and the Federal Reserve's willingness to defend the currency's purchasing power — a promise, not a thing. That is the largest change in the history of money: for the first time, every currency on earth is a liability with no commodity at the bottom.

## Is the dollar's position weakening?

Slowly and partially. The dollar's share of allocated foreign-exchange reserves fell from a peak of about 71% in 2000 to 57.1% in the first quarter of 2026 (IMF COFER), with the euro at about 20%. But the decline has not gone to any single rival; it has gone to a spread of smaller currencies (Australian and Canadian dollars, Swiss franc, yuan) and, above all, to gold. The ECB reported in June 2026 that gold reached 27% of global central-bank reserves at end-2025, up from 20% a year earlier, overtaking both the euro (15%) and US Treasuries (22%) as a reserve asset, though dollar-denominated assets of all kinds remained the largest single category at 42%.

The catalyst was the freezing of about $300 billion of Russian central-bank reserves by the US and EU in February 2022. Every reserve manager drew the lesson that dollar reserves are, in the last resort, a claim that the issuer can cancel, whereas gold in one's own vault is not. Central banks bought over 1,000 tonnes a year in 2022, 2023 and 2024, 850–863 tonnes in 2025, and about 57 and 289 tonnes in the first two quarters of 2026 (the Q1 figure was revised sharply down by the World Gold Council after reclassifying some purchases as over-the-counter), led by China, Poland, India, Turkey, Uzbekistan and Kazakhstan. This is the de-dollarization that is actually happening: not a move to the yuan, but a partial move back to the one asset that predates the dollar.

Yet in trade invoicing, bank lending, foreign-exchange turnover (the dollar is on one side of about 88% of all FX trades) and bond issuance, the dollar's share has barely moved, because those are the network-effect uses, and there is no alternative with the same depth. Gold cannot invoice a shipment or fund a bank; the euro lacks a unified bond market and fiscal backstop; the yuan is not convertible. The dollar's role as the world's unit of account and settlement currency is therefore intact, while its role as the world's reserve *store of value* is being shared again with gold.

## Key takeaways

The dollar replaced gold because in 1944 it was the only credible proxy for gold, because oil and then all commodities were priced in it, because the Treasury and Eurodollar markets gave it unmatched liquidity, and because a fiat currency can expand, earn interest and settle instantly where gold cannot. Gold is not the unit of account because nothing is priced in it — gold itself is priced in dollars. Since 2022 central banks have been rebalancing reserves from dollars into gold, and gold now exceeds the euro and US Treasuries as a share of reserves, but the dollar's dominance in pricing, trade and finance remains unchallenged.
