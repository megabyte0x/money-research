# 05 — Silver, Copper, Bronze and Bimetallism: How the Other Metals Fit Under Gold

## Silver was the workhorse, not the understudy

The popular image is gold first, then silver as a cheaper substitute. The record is nearly the reverse. Silver was the unit of account in Mesopotamia from about 3000 BCE, two and a half millennia before any gold coin. The Greek world ran on silver drachmas and tetradrachms; the Roman Republic on the silver denarius (from 211 BCE); early medieval Europe for five hundred years on the silver penny alone; India on the silver rupee from Sher Shah Suri (1540) to 1898; China on silver ingots (*sycee*) and Spanish dollars until 1935; Mexico on the silver peso; and the United States, in law, on a bimetallic dollar defined in both silver and gold until 1873 and in practice on silver-based coinage for most of its first century. The word for "money" in French (*argent*), Spanish (*plata*, colloquially), Hebrew (*kesef*) and many other languages is the word for silver.

The reason is arithmetic. A gold coin of useful size — 4 to 8 grams — was worth weeks of a labourer's wages. It could not buy bread. Silver at roughly one-tenth to one-fifteenth the value per gram made coins that matched daily transactions, and copper or bronze filled in below that. So the three metals formed a ladder: gold for the state, the merchant and the hoard; silver for wages, rents, taxes and markets; copper for small change. Gold sat at the top of the ladder not because it was used most but because it was the tier that everything else was ultimately measured against.

## Copper and bronze: the oldest and the smallest

Copper was the first metal humans smelted and the first used as money by weight (Egyptian *deben* of copper, Mesopotamian copper prices). Rome's earliest money was rough bronze lumps (*aes rude*), then cast bronze bars and the heavy *aes grave* coins; the Latin word for money, *pecunia*, comes from *pecus*, cattle, and *aes* meant bronze. China's cash coins were cast bronze with a square hole, strung in strings of a thousand, from 221 BCE to 1911. Sweden in the seventeenth century issued copper plate money weighing up to 20 kg because its copper mines were richer than its silver.

But copper's abundance means copper coins were almost always *token* coins: worth more as coins than as metal, and accepted because the state fixed their value against silver and gold. This created the "big problem of small change" (the title of Sargent and Velde's 2002 history): when copper was too valuable, small coins were melted; when too cheap, they were counterfeited and inflated. It was not solved until the nineteenth century, when states learned to issue limited quantities of token coins and stand ready to convert them.

## Bimetallism: the fixed ratio and why it kept failing

From Croesus onward, most monetary systems fixed a legal exchange rate between gold and silver coins. Croesus used about 13.3:1; Persia about 13:1; Rome under Augustus 12.5:1 (1 aureus = 25 denarii); the early Caliphate about 7:1 by metal weight, drifting toward 10:1; Newton's Britain 15.2:1; the US Coinage Act of 1792 15:1 and of 1834 16:1; the Latin Monetary Union of 1865 15.5:1.

The trouble is that the *market* ratio moves whenever a new mine opens or trade shifts. When the legal ratio undervalues one metal, that metal is worth more as bullion abroad than as coin at home, so it is exported or melted and vanishes from circulation, leaving only the overvalued metal — Gresham's law, "bad money drives out good." Every bimetallic system in history alternated between de facto gold and de facto silver as the ratio swung:

Sixteenth-century Europe was flooded with Potosí silver after 1545, so silver cheapened against gold and Europe tilted toward silver.

Newton's 1717 rate overvalued gold in Britain, so silver flowed out to the Continent and India, where it bought more; Britain drifted onto gold without ever deciding to.

The US 15:1 ratio of 1792 undervalued gold, so gold coins disappeared and the early US was effectively on silver; the 16:1 ratio of 1834 overvalued gold, and after the Californian discoveries of 1848 silver coins disappeared and the US was effectively on gold.

The 1870s reversed everything: Germany, flush with French war indemnity gold, demonetized silver in 1871–73; the US quietly dropped the silver dollar in the Coinage Act of 1873 (the "Crime of '73"); the Comstock Lode and other Nevada strikes then poured silver onto a market with fewer buyers, and the ratio slid from 15.5:1 to over 30:1 by 1900. Silver-mining states and indebted farmers fought for its remonetization — William Jennings Bryan's "Cross of Gold" speech of 1896 — and lost. India, whose rupee was silver, saw its currency lose half its value against gold-standard Britain and closed its mints to free silver coinage in 1893.

## How silver and copper were "backed" by gold

Under a gold standard the answer is explicit: silver and copper coins were subsidiary tokens, legal tender only up to small amounts, issued in limited quantity, and convertible on demand into gold coin or notes at the mint or central bank. Their value came from the promise of conversion, not from their metal. Under bimetallism the answer is that both metals were "full-bodied" — worth their weight — and the fixed ratio was a rule about how the mint would exchange them, not a backing.

Paper money is the same principle one step further. The Song dynasty's *jiaozi* (1024) were receipts for strings of bronze cash held by merchants; when the Yuan dynasty issued unbacked paper and printed too much, it inflated to nothing, and Ming China went back to silver. In Europe goldsmith-bankers' receipts (seventeenth-century London) became banknotes; the Bank of England (1694), the Bank of Amsterdam and later every central bank held gold and silver and issued notes redeemable in it. The metal was the reserve; the paper was the circulating medium. The whole modern financial system is that structure with the gold removed from the bottom in 1971.

| 1980 | ~17 : 1 | Hunt brothers' silver corner |
| 2020s | roughly 70–100 : 1 | free-floating |

The ratio's drift from ~13 to ~80 over 2,500 years is the quantitative trace of silver's demotion from money to mostly industrial metal.

## Key takeaways

Silver, not gold, was the everyday money of most of history; copper and bronze handled small change; gold sat at the top as the reserve and large-value unit. Fixed gold–silver ratios (bimetallism) were tried continuously from Croesus to 1873 and always broke when mine discoveries moved the market ratio, via Gresham's law. Silver and copper coins were "backed" by gold only in the sense that the state promised to exchange them at a fixed rate; paper money began as a receipt for metal and kept that form until 1971.
