# 01 — The Break, 1971–1976: From the Nixon Shock to Legalised Floating

## What actually happened on 15 August 1971

Nixon's Sunday-night broadcast from Camp David announced three things at once, and the gold suspension was the one he mentioned last. First, a 90-day freeze on wages and prices — the first peacetime price controls in the United States. Second, a 10% surcharge on all dutiable imports, explicitly to force other countries to revalue their currencies against the dollar. Third, the "temporary" suspension of the dollar's convertibility into gold for foreign central banks. The package was designed by Treasury Secretary John Connally and Under-Secretary Paul Volcker; Connally's summary to European finance ministers that autumn — "the dollar is our currency, but it's your problem" — became the motto of the fiat era.

The immediate cause was a run. Foreign official dollar holdings were roughly four times the US gold stock, Britain had asked to convert $3 billion the week before, and France had been converting steadily since 1965. The deeper cause was that Bretton Woods had asked the United States to run the world's reserve currency and fight the Vietnam War and build the Great Society at the same time, with money supply growing faster than gold could ever be mined.

## The Smithsonian Agreement (December 1971)

For four months the world's exchange rates simply floated while governments negotiated. The Group of Ten met at the Smithsonian Institution in Washington on 17–18 December 1971 and agreed the first multilateral realignment of currencies in history: the dollar was devalued about 8% (the official gold price went from $35 to $38 an ounce, though the gold window stayed shut, so the price was fictional), the yen was revalued by about 17% and the Deutsche Mark by about 13.5%, and the permitted fluctuation band was widened from ±1% to ±2.25%. Nixon called it "the most significant monetary agreement in the history of the world." It lasted fourteen months.

The agreement failed for the reason Bretton Woods had failed: the US kept running deficits and expanding money into the 1972 election, dollars kept piling up abroad, and speculators knew a second devaluation was coming. In February 1973 the dollar was devalued again, to $42.22 per ounce; in March 1973 the European Community's six members and Japan stopped defending their rates and let their currencies float against the dollar. From March 1973 the world was on floating exchange rates by default, without any treaty saying so.

## Europe tries to keep fixed rates among itself

Europe never accepted floating as a permanent condition. In April 1972 the six EC members plus Britain, Ireland, Denmark and Norway created the "snake" — currencies fixed to each other within a ±2.25% band while floating jointly against the dollar. Britain left after six weeks, Italy in 1973, France in 1974 and again in 1976; by 1977 the snake was effectively a Deutsche Mark zone of Germany, the Benelux countries and Denmark. Its successor, the European Monetary System of March 1979 with its Exchange Rate Mechanism, was the direct ancestor of the euro (file 04). The whole European project of the next thirty years can be read as an attempt to rebuild Bretton Woods on a continental scale with the Bundesbank playing the role of the gold window.

## The oil shock forces the issue (1973–74)

The Yom Kippur War of October 1973 and the OPEC embargo quadrupled the price of oil within months (file 02). Fixed rates could not survive a shock that transferred hundreds of billions of dollars from importers to a handful of exporters within a year; the balance-of-payments swings were too large for any parity to hold. The oil shock therefore killed any prospect of returning to Bretton Woods, and it explains why the floating that began in 1973 was never reversed.

## Herstatt and the birth of banking rules (1974)

On 26 June 1974 the German regulators closed Bankhaus Herstatt, a Cologne bank that had lost heavily speculating on the newly floating currencies, at the end of the German business day — after counterparties had paid their Deutsche Marks but before Herstatt had delivered the dollars in New York. Banks around the world were left unpaid; the dollar payment system in New York seized for days. "Herstatt risk," the risk that one leg of a foreign-exchange trade settles and the other does not, was a phenomenon that could not exist under fixed rates and only became a problem when currencies moved. The G10 central bank governors responded by creating, at the end of 1974, the Basel Committee on Banking Supervision, which has written every international bank-capital rule since — Basel I (1988), II (2004) and III (2010 onward). The fiat era's regulatory architecture was born from the first fiat-era bank failure.

## Legalising the accident: the Jamaica Accords (1976)

The IMF's Committee of Twenty spent 1972–74 trying to design a reformed fixed-rate system and gave up. In January 1976, at Kingston, Jamaica, the IMF's Interim Committee agreed the Second Amendment to the Articles of Agreement, which came into force on 1 April 1978. It did four things that still define the international monetary system:

It legalised floating. Members could choose any exchange arrangement they liked — float, peg to another currency, peg to a basket, or join a currency union — but not peg to gold.

It demonetised gold. The official price of gold was abolished, members were no longer required to hold or pay in gold, the IMF sold one-sixth of its gold on the market (with the profits going to developing countries) and returned another sixth to members, and the Special Drawing Right was declared the "principal reserve asset" of the system — a declaration that never came true.

It gave the IMF a new job: "firm surveillance" of members' exchange-rate policies instead of administration of fixed parities. This is the origin of the IMF's modern role as monitor, lender of last resort and enforcer of conditionality in the crises of the 1980s and 1990s (files 03 and 05).

It left the dollar where it was. Nothing in the amendment made the dollar the reserve currency; nothing needed to. It was already what everyone held, invoiced in and settled in.

In the United States, private ownership of gold became legal again on 31 December 1974, and the Treasury and IMF gold auctions of 1975–79 were partly intended to show that gold was now just a commodity. The market disagreed: gold rose from $42 in 1971 to $195 by the end of 1974 and, after a dip, to $850 in January 1980.

## What floating did to the world economy

Exchange rates turned out to be far more volatile than the economists who had advocated floating (Friedman's 1953 essay is the classic) had expected. The dollar–Deutsche Mark rate moved 20–30% within single years in the 1970s and 1980s. This had four lasting consequences. Firms and banks had to hedge, which created a demand that new financial instruments rushed to meet: currency futures at the Chicago Mercantile Exchange in May 1972, listed options at the Chicago Board Options Exchange in April 1973 (the same month the Black–Scholes pricing paper appeared), and eventually the swap and derivatives industry (file 08). Foreign-exchange trading became a business in itself; daily turnover grew from perhaps $10–20 billion in 1973 to $1.2 trillion by 1995 and about $7.5 trillion by 2022. Countries lost the "discipline" of gold and had to invent a replacement, which became the story of the next decade (file 02). And international coordination moved from a rulebook to a summit: the first G6 leaders' summit met at Rambouillet in November 1975 largely to bless the Jamaica deal, and it has met every year since as the G7.

## Key takeaways

The Nixon shock was a wage freeze, an import tariff and a gold suspension in one package, aimed at forcing allies to revalue. The Smithsonian Agreement tried to rebuild fixed rates at a lower dollar and collapsed within fourteen months; the 1973 oil shock made a return impossible. Floating happened by default in March 1973 and was made legal by the Jamaica Accords of 1976, which also demonetised gold and gave the IMF its surveillance role. Europe alone refused floating and began building what became the euro. The first casualties of volatile currencies — Herstatt in 1974 — created the Basel Committee and the entire framework of international bank regulation.
