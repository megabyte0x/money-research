# 02 — Before Money: Ornament, Ritual, Power and Other Uses

For roughly four thousand years — from Varna (~4600 BCE) to Lydia (~650 BCE) — gold was worked, traded, hoarded and fought over without being money in the modern sense. Understanding what it did in that period explains why it was later so easily accepted as money: it was already the most universally desired object in the ancient world.

## Ornament and status

Gold's first and most enduring use is adornment. At Varna the gold was beads, bracelets, pectorals, sceptre fittings and, in Grave 43, a penis sheath — items of display on a single high-status male, buried with him. The message was rank. The pattern repeats everywhere: the Royal Cemetery of Ur in Sumer (~2600–2500 BCE) with Queen Puabi's gold headdress, the Mycenaean shaft graves with the "Mask of Agamemnon" (~1550 BCE), Egyptian collars and pectorals, Scythian animal-style goldwork, the gold of the Indus cities, the Moche and Chimú of Peru. Jewellery was not decorative fluff; it was wealth worn on the body, portable, visible, and inheritable, which is exactly why jewellery is still the single largest use of gold today (about 1,540 tonnes in 2025).

## Religion and the divine

In Egypt gold was "the flesh of the gods," specifically of the sun-god Ra; it did not tarnish, so it was the material of eternity. Tutankhamun's innermost coffin (~1323 BCE) is about 110 kg of solid gold and his funerary mask about 10 kg. Temples were gilded, statues of gods were made of or plated with gold, and Egyptian pharaohs sent gold as diplomatic gifts to Near Eastern kings, who complained in the Amarna letters that "gold in Egypt is as common as dust." In Mesopotamia and later Israel, sanctuaries and cult objects (the Ark, the Menorah) were of gold. In India gold is associated with Lakshmi and prosperity and is still bought on auspicious days; in Buddhist Southeast Asia, gold leaf is pressed onto stupas and images. The link between gold and the sacred is one reason gold was hoarded by temples, and temples were among the first "banks" — in Babylon, Greece (Delphi, Delos) and Rome (the Temple of Saturn held the state treasury, the *aerarium*).

## Burial and the afterlife

Because gold does not corrode, it was the natural material for the grave. Varna, Ur, Mycenae, Egypt, the Scythian kurgans, the Anglo-Saxon Sutton Hoo ship (~625 CE), the Sican tombs of Peru — a huge share of the gold we have from antiquity comes out of tombs. Burying gold also removed it from circulation, which mattered later when it was money: an enormous amount of the ancient world's gold was simply taken out of the economy and put underground.

## Tribute, gifts and plunder

Before markets, gold moved between rulers as tribute (Nubian gold to Egypt, Lydian gold to Persia), as gifts sealing alliances, as ransom, and as loot. Croesus of Lydia sent gold ingots and a gold lion to the oracle at Delphi. Alexander seized the Persian treasuries at Susa and Persepolis (reported at 180,000 talents of gold and silver), which immediately became coin. Rome's conquest of Dacia in 106 CE yielded so much gold that Trajan paid a cash gift of 650 denarii to every citizen and staged 123 days of games. This "state-to-state" gold was, in effect, gold functioning as reserve money before there was a monetary system to name it as such.

## Practical and technical uses

Gold's malleability gave it uses beyond display. Gilding — applying gold leaf or, later, mercury-amalgam fire-gilding — covered wood, bronze, stone and manuscripts. Gold thread was woven into textiles from the Bronze Age onward. The Etruscans (~700 BCE) made dental appliances of gold wire and bands, the beginning of gold dentistry, which remained standard until the twentieth century because gold does not corrode in the mouth. In Chinese and Indian medicine and in European alchemy, gold was ingested as *aurum potabile*, "drinkable gold," on the theory that an incorruptible metal must confer incorruptibility; alchemy's central project, transmuting base metal into gold, drove a great deal of early chemistry. In the modern era gold's conductivity and corrosion resistance put it in every electronic connector and, in trace amounts, in cancer drugs, spacecraft shielding and the James Webb telescope's mirrors; technology consumed about 323 tonnes in 2025.

## A unit of weight before a unit of money

Egypt priced goods in *deben* (about 91 g) of copper, silver or gold and paid with rings, bars and dust weighed on a balance, without coins. Mesopotamia reckoned in the silver *shekel* (about 8.3 g), and the Code of Hammurabi (~1750 BCE) sets fines in silver; gold appears in Mesopotamian records but priced in silver, at roughly 8–10 units of silver to one of gold. The Hebrew *shekel*, the Greek *talent* and *mina*, and the Roman *libra* are all weights before they are coins. This is the crucial bridge: for two thousand years metal was money-by-weight, and the balance scale, not the mint, was the instrument of trade.

## Key takeaways

Gold was already the most desired substance on earth — for beauty, permanence, sacredness and rank — long before it was spent. It was used for jewellery, temples, tombs, diplomacy, gilding, textiles, dentistry and medicine, and it moved between rulers as tribute and plunder. When coinage arrived, gold did not have to earn acceptance; it only had to be standardized.
