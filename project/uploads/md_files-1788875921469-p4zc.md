# After Gold: The Fiat World, 1971–2026 — Research Directory

Companion to the `gold-research/` set. That directory ends on 15 August 1971, when the United States closed the gold window and, for the first time in history, no currency on earth was defined as a weight of anything. This directory asks what happened next: how a world with no metallic anchor organised its money, what rules and treaties replaced the gold standard, how wars were financed and fought with money itself as a weapon, what new nations and new currencies were born, what technologies changed the form of money, and how each of these shocks moved the world economy.

Compiled 8 September 2026. Historical material is drawn from standard economic-history sources; present-day figures come from the IMF, the ECB, the World Gold Council, central-bank statements and press reports current to the first week of September 2026. Every file ends with a "Key takeaways" section so the arc can be skimmed by reading only those.

## The arc in one paragraph

When Nixon suspended gold convertibility in August 1971 he called it temporary; within five years the IMF had rewritten its articles to make floating currencies legal and gold officially irrelevant. The immediate result was the Great Inflation: without a metal to defend, central banks printed for growth, oil producers quadrupled prices twice, and by 1980 US inflation touched 14%, British inflation had passed 24% and gold had gone from $35 to $850. The dollar did not lose its role, because Saudi Arabia agreed to price oil in it and park the proceeds in Treasuries, and because Paul Volcker showed in 1979–82 that a fiat currency could be made credible by raising interest rates to 20% and accepting a deep recession. The lesson the world drew was that money would now be anchored not by gold but by independent central banks with inflation targets — an institutional design that spread from New Zealand in 1990 to almost every country by 2000, and was written into the treaty that created the euro. Freed from metal, finance exploded: currency futures, options, swaps, securitisation, electronic markets, the Eurodollar system and cross-border capital flows produced a crisis roughly every decade — Latin America 1982, Japan 1990, Europe 1992, Mexico 1994, Asia 1997, Russia and LTCM 1998, Argentina 2001, the world in 2008, the eurozone 2010–12 — each answered by a bigger bailout and a new rulebook (Basel I, II, III; Dodd-Frank; the ESM). The Cold War's end in 1989–91 created two dozen new states and currencies, most of which hyperinflated before stabilising on the same central-bank model. Wars in the fiat era — Yom Kippur, the Gulf, Iraq and Afghanistan, Ukraine, Gaza, Iran — were financed by borrowing rather than gold, and were fought increasingly with money itself: sanctions, SWIFT exclusions and, in 2022, the freezing of Russia's $300 billion in reserves. That act did what no rival currency could: it sent central banks back to gold, which by 2026 had overtaken the euro and US Treasuries as a share of world reserves. Meanwhile money dematerialised entirely — cards, mobile payments, Bitcoin, dollar stablecoins backed by Treasuries, and state digital currencies. In September 2026 the dollar still prices nearly everything; the world's central banks hold both Treasuries and record gold; a war in the Gulf has closed the Strait of Hormuz and produced the largest oil disruption ever recorded; and the question of what backs money is answered, as it has been since 1971, by trust in the institutions that issue it.
