# 11 — Glossary

**Alluvial / placer gold** — Gold eroded from rock and deposited in river gravel as dust, flakes and nuggets; gathered by panning or washing. The source of the earliest gold and of most gold rushes.

**Assay** — A test of a metal's purity. Ancient methods: touchstone streak, density; medieval and modern: fire assay (cupellation), acid test, X-ray fluorescence.

**Aureus** — Roman gold coin, ~8 g, regularized by Julius Caesar; 1 aureus = 25 silver denarii.

**Bezant** — Western European name for the Byzantine gold solidus/nomisma.

**Bimetallism** — A monetary system in which both gold and silver coins are full legal tender at a fixed legal ratio. Practised from Croesus (c. 550 BCE) to the 1870s.

**Bretton Woods system (1944–1971)** — Post-war order in which the dollar was fixed to gold at $35/oz and convertible for foreign governments, and all other currencies were pegged to the dollar.

**Bullion** — Gold or silver in bulk form (bars, ingots) valued by weight and purity rather than as coin.

**COFER** — IMF's Currency Composition of Official Foreign Exchange Reserves database.

**Croeseid** — First pure gold and pure silver coins, struck by Croesus of Lydia c. 550 BCE.

**Daric** — Persian gold coin of ~8.4 g introduced by Darius I; first empire-wide gold coin.

**Debasement** — Reducing the precious-metal content of a coin while keeping its face value; the classic way pre-modern states inflated.

**Deben** — Egyptian weight unit (~91 g) used to price goods in copper, silver or gold.

**Dinar** — Islamic gold coin of 4.25 g, from 696–697 CE; the word derives from the Roman *denarius*.

**Ducat** — Venetian gold coin of 3.5 g, struck unchanged from 1284 to 1797.

**Electrum** — Natural alloy of gold and silver (natural Pactolus electrum ~70–75% gold; Lydian coins ~54%); the metal of the first Lydian coins.

**ETF (gold)** — Exchange-traded fund holding physical gold; lets investors own gold as a security. ~800 t bought in 2025.

**Fiat money** — Currency with no commodity backing, whose value rests on the issuer's credibility and legal-tender laws. The global norm since 1971–73.

**Fineness / carat** — Purity of gold. 24 carat = pure (999+ fine); 22 carat = 916 fine (sovereigns, Indian jewellery); 18 carat = 750.

**Florin** — Florentine gold coin of 3.5 g, first struck 1252; restarted gold coinage in Western Europe.

**Gold-exchange standard** — A system in which a country's currency is convertible not into gold but into another currency that is (e.g. British India after 1898; the interwar system; Bretton Woods).

**Gold standard (classical)** — System (c. 1871–1914) in which each currency was defined as a fixed weight of gold, notes were redeemable in gold coin, and exchange rates were therefore fixed.

**Gold window** — The US Treasury's facility for converting foreign official dollars into gold at $35/oz; closed by Nixon on 15 August 1971.

**Good Delivery bar** — LBMA-standard bar of ~400 troy ounces (~12.4 kg), 995+ fine; the form in which central banks and bullion banks hold gold.

**Gresham's law** — "Bad money drives out good": when two monies are fixed at a legal ratio that differs from the market ratio, the undervalued one is hoarded or exported and disappears from circulation.

**Hacksilber** — Cut pieces of silver objects used as money by weight in the Bronze and Iron Age Near East.

**Legal tender** — Money that must by law be accepted in settlement of debts.

**LBMA** — London Bullion Market Association; sets the twice-daily benchmark gold price and bar standards.

**Lode / vein gold** — Gold within quartz veins in rock, requiring mining and crushing.

**Mithqal** — Weight of ~4.25 g used for gold in the Islamic and West African trade; equal to the dinar.

**Mohur** — Mughal gold coin of ~11 g.

**Petrodollar** — Dollars earned from oil sales, especially under the post-1974 arrangement by which OPEC oil was priced in dollars and proceeds invested in US assets.

**Price-specie flow mechanism** — David Hume's account of how a gold standard self-corrects: trade deficits export gold, shrink the money supply, lower prices, and restore competitiveness.

**Reserve asset / reserve currency** — Assets held by central banks to back their currency and settle international obligations; a reserve currency is one widely held for that purpose.

**SDR (Special Drawing Right)** — IMF synthetic reserve asset created 1969, valued against a basket of major currencies.

**Seigniorage** — The issuer's profit from creating money: the difference between a coin's face value and its metal cost, or the interest saved by issuing non-interest-bearing currency.

**Shekel** — Mesopotamian weight (~8.3 g) and later coin; the basic silver unit of account of the ancient Near East.

**Solidus** — Constantine's gold coin of 4.5 g (72 to the Roman pound), 309/312 CE; stable for ~700 years.

**Sovereign** — British gold coin of 7.32 g fine gold (7.988 g at 22 carat), defined 1816–17; still struck.

**Stater** — Generic Greek/Lydian term for a standard coin, gold or silver.

**Stock-to-flow** — Ratio of existing above-ground stock to annual new supply; for gold about 60:1, meaning new mining adds ~1.5–2% a year.

**Token coin (subsidiary coin)** — A coin whose face value exceeds its metal value, accepted because the state limits its issue and converts it on demand; how silver and copper coins worked under a gold standard.

**Touchstone** — Fine-grained dark stone on which gold is rubbed; the colour of the streak, compared to reference needles, indicates purity. In use from the Lydian period.

**Triffin dilemma** — Robert Triffin's 1960 observation that a reserve-currency issuer must run deficits to supply the world with reserves, which eventually undermines confidence in its convertibility.

**Troy ounce** — 31.1035 g; the unit in which gold is priced.

**Unit of account** — The unit in which prices, wages and debts are expressed. Distinct from medium of exchange (what is handed over) and store of value (what is held).
