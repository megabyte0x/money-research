# 07 — The Gold Standard Era, 1717–1971: How Gold Became the Sole World Money and How It Lost the Job

## Newton's accident (1717)

Britain in 1700 was bimetallic and losing its silver: the Continent and India valued silver more highly, so English silver coins were exported and melted. Isaac Newton, as Master of the Mint, was asked to fix the guinea's silver price. He set it at 21 shillings, which at the market rates of the day slightly *overvalued* gold (about 15.2:1). The predictable result — Gresham's law — was that gold flowed into Britain, mostly Brazilian gold via Portugal, and silver continued to flow out. Newton had not intended a gold standard, but by the 1770s Britain was on one in practice. Parliament limited silver's legal-tender status in 1774, and the Coinage Act of 1816 made it official: gold was the sole standard, the new *sovereign* was defined as 7.32 g of fine gold (about £3 17s 10½d per ounce, a price that would hold, with wartime interruptions, until 1931), and silver became subsidiary token coinage. After the Napoleonic wars' paper-money interlude (1797–1821), the Bank of England resumed gold payments in 1821.

Britain was the first modern country on a pure gold standard, and for the next half-century it was alone. France, the US, the German states, the Latin countries, Russia, India and China were all on silver or bimetallism.

## The gold rushes (1848–1900)

Gold discoveries in California (1848), Australia (1851), New Zealand (1861), South Africa's Witwatersrand (1886), the Klondike (1896) and Western Australia (1890s) roughly tripled the world's gold stock in fifty years. World output, which had been perhaps 20–30 tonnes a year in 1800, was 300 tonnes a year by 1900 and 700 tonnes by 1912; South Africa alone became the largest producer, a position it held until 2006. This mattered in two ways: it made a gold-only world money physically possible (before 1850 there was simply not enough gold to run the world's commerce), and it made silver relatively more abundant when the Nevada and Mexican strikes came, which pushed governments to choose gold.

## The classical gold standard (1871–1914)

The turning point was the Franco-Prussian War. France paid Germany an indemnity of 5 billion francs, much of it in gold, and the new German Empire used it to adopt the gold mark in 1871–73 and sell its silver. The Scandinavian countries followed (1873–75), the Netherlands (1875), France and the Latin Monetary Union suspended free silver coinage (1873–78), the US demonetized the silver dollar (Coinage Act of 1873) and resumed gold payments in 1879, Austria-Hungary (1892), Russia and Japan (1897), India via a gold-exchange standard (1898), and the US formally with the Gold Standard Act of 1900 at $20.67 per ounce. By 1900 nearly every major economy defined its currency as a weight of gold. China was the great holdout on silver.

Mechanics: each currency was a fixed weight of gold, so exchange rates were fixed (£1 = $4.8665 for a century), gold coin circulated alongside notes redeemable in gold, and central banks held gold reserves against their note issue. Trade imbalances were supposed to settle themselves by the "price-specie flow mechanism" — a deficit country lost gold, its money supply and prices fell, its exports became competitive — though in practice the Bank of England managed the system by moving its discount rate. Capital flowed freely, prices were stable over decades (though with sharp deflations in the 1870s–90s that ruined farmers and debtors), and London was the clearing house of the world. This was the only period in history when gold alone was the unit of account for the whole world economy, and it lasted about forty years.

## The wars break it (1914–1944)

Every belligerent suspended gold convertibility in August 1914 to print money for the war. Afterward the attempt to restore the pre-war system failed. Britain returned to gold in 1925 at the pre-war parity, which Keynes argued overvalued the pound by about 10% and forced deflation and unemployment; it left gold again in September 1931 in the middle of the banking crisis. The interwar "gold exchange standard" let central banks hold pounds and dollars as reserves instead of gold, which made the system fragile. Under the gold standard's rules, countries fighting the Depression could not expand money without losing gold, so the standard transmitted deflation from country to country; the economies that left gold earliest (Britain 1931, the US 1933) recovered earliest, those that stayed longest (France's "gold bloc," to 1936) suffered longest.

In the United States, Franklin Roosevelt's Executive Order 6102 of April 1933 required citizens to surrender gold coin and bullion to the Federal Reserve at $20.67 per ounce; the Gold Reserve Act of January 1934 then devalued the dollar to $35 per ounce, a 41% devaluation and a windfall to the Treasury. Private ownership of monetary gold remained illegal for Americans until 31 December 1974. Gold coins stopped circulating in almost every country in the 1930s; from then on gold was held only by central banks and treasuries, and "the gold standard" meant a standard for governments, not for people.

## Bretton Woods (1944–1971)

In July 1944, forty-four Allied nations at Bretton Woods, New Hampshire, designed the post-war order. The US held about two-thirds of the world's monetary gold — roughly 20,000 tonnes — so the system was built around the dollar: the dollar was fixed to gold at $35 per ounce and convertible into gold *for foreign governments and central banks only*; every other currency was fixed to the dollar with a ±1% band, adjustable in case of "fundamental disequilibrium"; and the International Monetary Fund was created to lend to countries in balance-of-payments trouble. Gold was still the anchor, but the dollar was the working currency, and other countries increasingly held dollars rather than gold as reserves because dollars earned interest.

The flaw was identified by economist Robert Triffin in 1960: for the world to have enough dollar reserves to grow, the US had to run persistent deficits, but persistent deficits meant that foreign dollar holdings would eventually exceed US gold, and the promise to convert at $35 would become incredible. By the late 1950s foreign dollar claims already exceeded US gold; the US gold stock fell from over 20,000 tonnes in the early 1950s to about 9,000 by 1971, and to 8,133 after the sales of the 1970s, where it remains. The London Gold Pool (1961–68), a consortium of eight central banks selling gold to hold the market price at $35, lost France in June 1967 and collapsed in March 1968 when the run that followed sterling's November 1967 devaluation overwhelmed it; a two-tier market followed, with an official $35 price and a free market price. Charles de Gaulle called the dollar's position an "exorbitant privilege" and shipped US dollars home as gold. The IMF created Special Drawing Rights in 1969 as a synthetic reserve asset ("paper gold").

## The Nixon shock (15 August 1971)

With Vietnam War spending and inflation draining gold, and Britain and France asking to convert large dollar balances, President Nixon on 15 August 1971 "temporarily" suspended the dollar's convertibility into gold. It was never restored. The Smithsonian Agreement of December 1971 repriced gold at $38 and then $42.22 (1973), but the fixed rates collapsed and by March 1973 the major currencies were floating. The IMF's Jamaica Accords (January 1976, effective 1978) formally removed gold from the international monetary system: currencies would no longer be defined in gold, and the IMF sold or returned a third of its gold. The US official gold price of $42.22 still exists as a bookkeeping value for the Treasury's 8,133 tonnes, a relic.

From August 1971 to January 1980 the free-market gold price rose from $35 to $850 as inflation, oil shocks and the loss of the anchor drove investors to the metal. Nothing has been money by law with a gold definition since.

## Why the system failed: the underlying tension

A gold standard promises price stability by tying money to a metal whose supply grows about 1.5–2% a year. That is also its weakness: in a world where economies grow faster and governments want to fight wars and depressions, the fixed supply becomes a straitjacket. Every crisis of the twentieth century — 1914, 1931, 1933, 1968, 1971 — was resolved by governments choosing to break the gold link rather than accept the deflation and unemployment the link demanded. The gold standard did not fail technically; it was abandoned because democratic states would not accept its discipline, and because the country at the centre, the United States, found that it could substitute its own liabilities for gold and the rest of the world would accept them. ## Key takeaways

Britain fell onto a gold standard by Newton's 1717 mispricing and legalized it in 1816; the gold rushes made a gold-only world money possible; the 1870s saw every major economy demonetize silver and fix its currency to gold; the classical gold standard ran from about 1871 to 1914 and was the only period when gold alone was the world's unit of account. The world wars and the Depression broke it, Bretton Woods in 1944 replaced it with a dollar convertible into gold for governments only, Triffin's dilemma made that unsustainable, and Nixon ended it on 15 August 1971. Since 1976 gold has had no legal monetary role anywhere.
