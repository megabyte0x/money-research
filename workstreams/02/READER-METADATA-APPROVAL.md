## gold-01

Question: What about gold made it usable, and where does the earliest evidence come from?

Short answer: Gold is durable, workable and sometimes found in native form. The Varna cemetery is an important early known gold assemblage, but physical properties alone did not make gold money.

Takeaways:
1. Gold's chemistry helps explain preservation and working.
2. Placer and lode sources involve different extraction.
3. Varna is an early securely dated gold assemblage, not proof that gold was the first worked metal.
4. Above-ground stock estimates depend on the World Gold Council's stock definition and report vintage.

Evidence/uncertainty: The Varna municipal archaeological guide, pp. 1–2, supports the cemetery's dating and object-count range. The buried metal's precise provenance and early electrum fineness still need scholarship. Stock estimates require a dated observation with its category definition.

## gold-02

Question: What was gold used for before coins?

Short answer: Archaeological objects and historical records show ornament, ritual, royal treasure, diplomatic gifts and some weighed-metal use in particular societies.

Takeaways:
1. Grave goods establish objects; motives require interpretation.
2. Temple storage and lending roles varied rather than following one first-bank model.
3. Ancient treasure totals are chronicle claims, not audited datasets.
4. Weighed metal and coinage are different transactions.

Evidence/uncertainty: World Gold Council full-year 2025 table 1 distinguishes jewellery consumption from fabrication and records greater investment demand than either jewellery measure that year. Exact archaeological or textual locators remain missing for Tutankhamun weights, Alexander's talents, Trajan's payouts, ancient metal ratios and medical firsts. The modern category comparison does not verify those ancient claims or a timeless ranking.

## gold-03

Question: What changed when weighed metal became stamped coin?

Short answer: A stamp identified an issuer and denomination and could lower verification costs, but early electrum composition varied and a coin's purchasing power still depended on acceptance.

Takeaways:
1. Units of account existed before coins.
2. Weighed bullion could require scales or testing to establish its quantity and quality.
3. Croesus' separate gold and silver issues were an important change.
4. A legal exchange ratio differs from redemption and market value.

Evidence/uncertainty: A numismatic catalogue or excavated-issue locator is still needed for coin dates, weights, purity and exact equivalences. The Croeseid ten-for-one relation, daric/siglos equivalence and a first-bimetallic-standard claim are withheld; this note does not verify them.

## gold-04

Question: Why does gold have value?

Short answer: Physical uses and monetary properties matter, but its price also reflects social acceptance, institutions, expectations and market conditions.

Takeaways:
1. Menger's saleability theory is one interpretation of money's emergence.
2. Credit and state-account theories offer competing interpretations, without settling the archaeological record.
3. A physical bar held outright is not an issuer's liability, but it has custody and price risk.
4. Gold's monetary use was never universal across societies.

Evidence/uncertainty: Direct locators for the competing theory texts and matched inflation, crisis and return data are still missing. The theories are interpretations, not verified evidence badges or a guarantee of future hedging.

## gold-05

Question: Were other metals always backed by gold?

Short answer: No. Silver, copper and bronze could be full-bodied money, account units or subsidiary tokens under different laws; a mint ratio is not automatically a redemption promise.

Takeaways:
1. Some documented coinage systems authorised lower-denomination silver and copper coins alongside gold coins.
2. Legal mint ratios and market exchange ratios answer different questions.
3. A mismatch under a bimetallic rule could cause one coin type to disappear from ordinary circulation.
4. Ancient reconstructed ratios and modern market quotes cannot be published as one measured series.

Evidence/uncertainty: The US Coinage Act of 1792, §9, supports one lower-denomination issue design, not measured coin circulation or transaction frequencies. Exact statute locators are still needed for later US and Latin Monetary Union mint ratios. Ancient reconstructed ratios and a consistently defined modern market series remain unverified. No ancient legal-ratio fact badge or continuous silver-demotion trend is approved.

## gold-06

Question: Did empires use gold in the same way?

Short answer: No. Gold served coin, treasury, gift and wealth roles in different states, while silver, copper, paper and credit often handled accounting or payments.

Takeaways:
1. India and China do not fit a universal European sequence of metallic money.
2. Ancient treasure totals and reported price effects do not establish one causal rule across empires.
3. A coin, treasury holding and gift can give gold different roles within one polity.

Evidence/uncertainty: Issue and assay catalogues, medieval quantity sources, Mansa Musa price evidence and country-specific coin laws still need dated exact locators. Persian/Byzantine trading extent and a numerical third-century Roman fineness series are withheld from reader takeaways.

## gold-07

Question: Which gold standards existed, and what changed in 1971?

Short answer: Britain's adoption, the classical international standard, interwar restorations and Bretton Woods had distinct convertibility rules; the US suspended official dollar-to-gold conversion in 1971.

Takeaways:
1. A gold definition did not eliminate bank credit.
2. Scholarship associates earlier departure from gold with earlier Depression recovery; this is not a complete one-cause explanation.
3. Bretton Woods official conversion differed from domestic gold-coin circulation.
4. The IMF's 1978 reform did not abolish official reserve gold.

Evidence/uncertainty: NBER Eichengreen/Sachs W1498 and Bernanke W4814 support the scoped interwar association. IMF *Financial Organization and Operations*, ch. III p. 91, and the 1964 US policy task-force “Money” report, printed pp. 44–47, support a conditional Bretton Woods liquidity and official gold-conversion tension. Country counts, historical gold-stock comparisons and a required trade-deficit or inevitable-run claim are not established.

## gold-08

Question: Why did dollar use survive official gold conversion?

Short answer: The dollar had established reserve, trade, banking and securities-market uses; oil invoicing and recycling contributed, but no reviewed 1974 document supplies a universal oil-redemption clause.

Takeaways:
1. Oil invoicing is not gold convertibility.
2. The US–Saudi joint commission addressed economic cooperation; it did not begin dollar oil quotations.
3. IMF COFER's foreign-exchange-reserve denominator excludes gold.
4. The ECB's broad-reserve gold-share increase was largely a valuation effect, not proof of active purchases.

Evidence/uncertainty: GAO ID-79-7, printed pp. 1–2, and the 1975 FRBNY staff memo, pp. 1–2, support the limited commission and invoicing claims. ECB June 2026 §1.1 chart 7 and IMF COFER metadata support the distinct denominator and valuation readings. A universal oil-pricing agreement, exact oil-recycling contribution and Treasury-market-size comparison remain unsupported.

## gold-09

Question: What can 2026 gold data actually establish?

Short answer: Dated World Gold Council, ECB and IMF observations establish prices, estimated flows and measured shares under specified conventions; they do not guarantee hedging or reveal every reserve manager's motives.

Takeaways:
1. World Gold Council demand excluding OTC/other differs from its total-demand measure.
2. The Council revised its central-bank-and-other-official-institutions net-demand estimate for the first quarter of 2026; the first release and revised vintage should not be conflated.
3. The ECB broad-reserve gold share and IMF COFER dollar foreign-exchange-reserve share use different denominators.
4. Basel bank credit and funding standards do not establish local central-bank reserve law or risk-free status.
5. A higher gold market-value share can result from price appreciation without proving active purchases.

Evidence/uncertainty: World Gold Council full-year 2025 table 1 and the first-quarter 2026 erratum support the category and revision distinctions; its full-year 2024 “Central banks” Notes and Definitions define the net-demand category to include other official-sector institutions. ECB June 2026 §1.1 chart 7, IMF COFER metadata and BIS CRE20.110/NSF30.31(4) support the scoped reserve and bank-rule claims. Use the existing observation records for quantities, dates and vintages in chapter body text; a revised second-quarter flow series, September spot quote, country holdings, asset-stock mix and matched return relationships still need primary-series review.
