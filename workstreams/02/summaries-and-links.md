## gold-01 — What about gold made it usable, and where does the earliest evidence come from?
Short answer: Gold is durable, workable and sometimes found in native form. The Varna cemetery is an important early known gold assemblage, but physical properties alone did not make gold money.
Takeaways: (1) Chemistry helps explain preservation and working. (2) Placer and lode sources involve different extraction. (3) Varna is an early securely dated assemblage, not proof that gold was the first worked metal. (4) Above-ground stock estimates depend on the WGC's stock definition and vintage.
Evidence/uncertainty: Varna municipal archaeological guide pp. 1–2 supports date and object-count range; precise provenance of the buried metal and early electrum fineness need scholarship.
Links: prerequisite gold-02, “Ornament and status” (what early objects actually did); deeper gold-03, “Stage two: the invention of the coin” (institutional step); counterargument gold-04, “Why one good becomes money at all” (social and credit theories).

## gold-02 — What was gold used for before coins?
Short answer: Archaeological objects and historical records show ornament, ritual, royal treasure, diplomatic gifts and some weighed-metal use in particular societies.
Takeaways: (1) Grave goods establish objects, while motives require interpretation. (2) Temples' storage/lending roles varied. (3) Ancient treasure totals are chronicle claims, not audited datasets. (4) Weighed metal and coinage are different transactions.
Evidence/uncertainty: WGC FY2025 table 1 distinguishes jewellery consumption 1,542.3 t from fabrication 1,638 t, and records investment demand of 2,175.3 t above either jewellery measure. Tutankhamun weights, Alexander's talents, Trajan's payouts, Egyptian/Mesopotamian ratios and medical firsts need exact archaeological/textual locators.
Links: prerequisite gold-01, “Where humans first found it”; deeper gold-03, “Stage one: metal by weight”; case gold-06, “Egypt (c. 3000–30 BCE)”.

## gold-03 — What changed when weighed metal became stamped coin?
Short answer: A stamp identified an issuer and denomination and could lower verification costs, but early electrum composition varied and a coin's purchasing power still depended on acceptance.
Takeaways: (1) Units of account existed before coins. (2) Weighed bullion required scale and assay. (3) Croesus' separate gold and silver issues were an important change. (4) A legal exchange ratio differs from redemption and market value.
Evidence/uncertainty: Coin dates, gold weights, purity, the Croeseid ten-to-one relation and daric/siglos equivalence need a numismatic catalogue or excavated-issue locator; the exact Croeseid equivalence was withheld from the body.
Links: prerequisite gold-02, “A unit of weight before a unit of money”; deeper gold-05, “Bimetallism: the fixed ratio and why it kept failing”; case gold-06, “Lydia and Persia (c. 650–330 BCE)”.

## gold-04 — Why does gold have value?
Short answer: Physical uses and monetary properties matter, but its price also reflects social acceptance, institutions, expectations and market conditions.
Takeaways: (1) Menger's saleability theory is one account of money's emergence. (2) Credit and state-account accounts offer alternatives. (3) A physical bar held outright is not an issuer's liability, but it has custody and price risk. (4) Monetary use was never universal across societies.
Evidence/uncertainty: The theory section needs direct Menger and competing-scholarship locators; universal return/hedge claims have been narrowed, but no matched return series is supplied.
Links: prerequisite gold-03, “Was gold ‘backed’ by anything?”; counterargument gold-05, “Silver was the workhorse, not the understudy”; deeper gold-09, “What still gives gold value”.

## gold-05 — Were other metals always backed by gold?
Short answer: No. Silver, copper and bronze could be full-bodied money, account units or subsidiary tokens under different laws; a mint ratio is not automatically a redemption promise.
Takeaways: (1) Some documented coinage systems authorised lower-denomination silver and copper coins alongside gold coins. (2) Legal and market ratios must be kept separate. (3) Bimetallic mismatch could cause one coin type to disappear from ordinary circulation. (4) The former ancient-to-modern ratio table cannot be published as one measured series.
Evidence/uncertainty: US Coinage Act 1792 §9 supports a lower-denomination issue design, not observed transaction frequency. US 1834 and LMU 1865 mint ratios need exact statute locators before observation promotion; modern market series and ancient reconstructed ratios remain unverified.
Links: prerequisite gold-03, “Stage three: Croesus separates gold from silver”; deeper gold-07, “The classical gold standard (1871–1914)”; case gold-06, “Japan (1600–1870)”.

## gold-06 — Did empires use gold in the same way?
Short answer: No. Gold served coin, treasury, gift and wealth roles in different states, while silver, copper, paper and credit often handled accounting or payments.
Takeaways: (1) Persian and Byzantine gold issues had important trading roles. (2) The third-century low-silver Roman issue was chiefly the antoninianus, not one continuous denarius series. (3) India and China do not fit a universal European ladder. (4) Ancient treasure totals and price effects should not support a single causal rule.
Evidence/uncertainty: Roman denomination/assay series, medieval gold quantities, Mansa Musa price effects and country-specific coin laws still need scholarship with dates and locators.
Links: prerequisite gold-03, “Stage four: coinage spreads”; case gold-06, “Rome (c. 300 BCE–476 CE)” and “China (c. 1000 BCE–1935)”; deeper gold-07, “The classical gold standard (1871–1914)”.

## gold-07 — Which gold standards existed, and what changed in 1971?
Short answer: Britain's adoption, the classical international standard, interwar restorations and Bretton Woods had distinct convertibility rules; the US suspended official dollar-to-gold conversion in 1971.
Takeaways: (1) A gold definition did not eliminate bank credit. (2) Earlier departure from gold is associated with earlier Depression recovery in scholarship, not a complete one-cause explanation. (3) Bretton Woods official conversion differs from domestic coin circulation. (4) The IMF's 1978 reform did not abolish reserve gold.
Evidence/uncertainty: NBER Eichengreen/Sachs W1498 and Bernanke W4814 support the scoped interwar association; IMF *Financial Organization and Operations*, ch. III p. 91, and the 1964 US policy task-force report, “Money,” printed pp. 44–47, support Triffin as a conditional Bretton Woods liquidity/conversion tension. Country-count and gold-stock numbers still need matched historical datasets.
Links: prerequisite gold-05, “How silver and copper were ‘backed’ by gold”; deeper gold-08, “Step one: the dollar becomes gold's proxy”; case gold-07, “The wars break it (1914–1944)”.

## gold-08 — Why did dollar use survive official gold conversion?
Short answer: The dollar had established reserve, trade, banking and securities-market uses; oil invoicing and recycling contributed, but no reviewed 1974 document supplies a universal oil-redemption clause.
Takeaways: (1) Invoicing is not convertibility. (2) The 1974 US–Saudi commission was economic cooperation, not the start of dollar oil quotations. (3) COFER's FX-reserve denominator excludes gold. (4) The ECB broad-reserve share increase was largely a valuation effect, not proof of active purchases.
Evidence/uncertainty: GAO ID-79-7 pp. 1–2, 1975 FRBNY memo pp. 1–2, ECB June 2026 §1.1 chart 7 and IMF COFER metadata support scoped claims; dollar-market-size and universal-trust claims are withheld or narrowed.
Links: prerequisite gold-07, “Bretton Woods (1944–1971)”; deeper gold-09, “The numbers”; counterargument gold-04, “Is gold's value ‘intrinsic’ or a social convention?”.

## gold-09 — What can 2026 gold data actually establish?
Short answer: Dated WGC, ECB and IMF observations establish prices, estimated flows and measured shares under specified conventions; they do not guarantee hedging or reveal every reserve manager's motives.
Takeaways: (1) WGC 2025 gold demand excluding OTC/other was 4,999 t, total demand 5,002 t. (2) Q1 2026 net demand in WGC's central-banks-and-other-official-institutions category was revised from 244 t to 57 t. (3) Gold's ECB broad-reserve share and dollar COFER FX share have different denominators. (4) Basel bank credit/funding standards do not establish local reserve law or risk-free status. (5) A high market-value share can result from price appreciation.
Evidence/uncertainty: WGC FY2025 table 1 and Q1 2026 erratum; WGC FY2024 “Central banks” Notes and Definitions for the wider official-institution net-demand category; ECB June 2026 §1.1 chart 7; IMF COFER 2026 Q1 brief; BIS CRE20.110/NSF30.31(4). Q2 2026 289 t, September spot quote, country holdings, asset stock mix and return relationships need exact primary-series review.
Links: prerequisite gold-08, “Is the dollar's position weakening?”; deeper gold-09, “Why it is still not ‘the’ unit of economy”; counterargument bitcoin-08, applicable reserve/regulation section after Bitcoin editor review.
