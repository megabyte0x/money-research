## Exact paragraph text and direct reading links

### Paragraph 1

> Money can serve several jobs. A unit of account states prices, wages or debts in a specified arrangement. A means of payment transfers value; final settlement depends on the arrangement. An asset may be held for later use, though its purchasing power can change. These jobs can belong to one instrument or separate. Holding gold or Bitcoin does not show that wages are priced in it; accepting a Bitcoin payment does not establish native tax, credit or wage denomination. A rising price over one period alone cannot establish reliable purchasing-power protection over another period or a general inflation/crisis hedge. Gold served ornament roles and, in some societies, weighed-metal roles; it later had coin and standard roles in particular regions and periods. The Bitcoin chapters test these roles separately for its protocol and surrounding institutions. Read [metal to money](/gold/03-from-metal-to-money-weights-rings-coins/) and [Bitcoin's demonstrated uses](/bitcoin/02-what-bitcoin-solved-and-what-it-did-not/).

### Paragraph 2

> Gold's monetary role depended on institutions as well as physical properties. Gold could be weighed and assayed, but those properties alone did not specify issuer liabilities. Issuing authorities set coin standards under particular laws; markets and users affected acceptance. In some documented coinage systems, lower-denomination silver and copper coins could serve smaller payments than gold coins. The [1792 US coinage law](https://www.usmint.gov/learn/history/historical-documents/coinage-act-of-april-2-1792), for example, specifies this denomination design; it does not measure actual circulation. Notes and bank deposits were distinct claims. In a specified gold-convertible arrangement, a note could promise redemption at a defined parity while banks issued claims against fractional reserves. If redeemable claims grew relative to reserves, meeting conversion requests could become difficult. Bimetallism and gold standards involved different rules and strains, not one universal metal-backed ladder. Read [other metals and fixed ratios](/gold/05-silver-copper-bronze-and-bimetallism/) and [the gold-standard era](/gold/07-the-gold-standard-era-1717-1971/).

### Paragraph 3

> On 15 August 1971 the United States suspended its Bretton Woods obligation to convert dollar holdings of foreign monetary authorities into gold. Paper money and bank credit already existed. Exchange-rate changes followed in stages; the IMF's later amendment changed international gold rules separately. Oil shocks overlapped with inflation already under way, so inflation cannot be assigned to the suspension alone. Triffin described a conditional Bretton Woods tension: if additional international reserves relied on foreign official dollar claims, those claims could supply liquidity while growing relative to US gold available for official conversion and straining confidence in that promise. This was no universal trade-deficit or inevitable-run law. Read [why the dollar displaced gold](/gold/08-why-the-dollar-replaced-gold/) and [the break after 1971](/after/01-the-break-1971-1976/).

### Paragraph 4

> The post-gold system relies on institutions with different jobs. Governments set legal obligations; central banks influence reserves and liquidity; commercial-bank lending creates deposits subject to capital, funding and borrower constraints. In the UK example, a customer payment instruction and interbank settlement of balances are distinct steps. The linked After Gold chapters examine selected debt and cross-border funding crises, the 2007–09 crash, and later asset purchases and emergency responses. They do not establish that each rule failed or any response delivered a measured benefit. Each response's size and effects need a defined period, measure and source. Read [managed-dollar crises](/after/03-debt-crises-and-the-managed-dollar-1982-1990/) and [the financial crisis and QE](/after/07-financial-crisis-and-the-age-of-qe-2007-2019/).

### Paragraph 5

> After the United States suspended official dollar-to-gold conversion and the IMF later ended the gold-based par-value rule, monetary authorities and households could still hold gold as an asset. A reserve holding or basket does not define wages, taxes and debts by a gold weight. At market value, a higher gold price can raise gold's reserve share without purchases or restored redemption. Gold's share of **broad official reserves**, which include gold, and a currency's share of **foreign-exchange reserves**, which exclude it, have different denominators. Trade invoicing, reserves, debt denomination and payments are separate measures; movement in one does not determine the others. Figures depend on the period, denominator and valuation convention; each needs a source. Read [gold's present role](/gold/09-gold-today-what-still-holds-its-value/) and [recent monetary shocks](/after/09-pandemic-inflation-and-weaponized-reserves-2020-2026/).

### Paragraph 6

> Bitcoin changes base-asset and validation rules. A validating node can check blocks and permitted subsidy under its chosen rules without one issuer's ledger. The schedule caps permitted issuance but does not measure inaccessible keys, liquid float or every subsidy claimed. These rules do not decide how custodians, lenders, merchants or laws operate. The cited Bitcoin chapters describe investment products and transfer mechanisms; they do not establish broad native Bitcoin wage, price or debt denomination in a large economy. A Bitcoin-denominated economy remains conditional: deposits and claims could change; redemption, reserves, lending, credit risk and velocity depend on its design. Fixed base issuance alone fixes neither broad money nor the price path. Read [the origin and design](/bitcoin/01-the-origin-what-2008-produced/) and [a Bitcoin unit of account](/bitcoin/07-the-supply-system-fixed-rate-variable-rate-and-backing/).

### Paragraph 7

> Bitcoin control depends on the action. Nodes validate against chosen rules; miners and pools construct blocks and affect inclusion; developers propose code; custodians can restrict client access; keys can be compromised or coerced; and authorities regulate within their reach. No single actor has the same power over validation, inclusion, key control and lawful access in every arrangement. Beneficial owners, ETF trusts, shareholders and custodians can describe overlapping interests in the same coins; mining templates differ from node validation. Country “adoption” likewise needs a named policy or legal act and responsible entity. Payment acceptance, seized or finally forfeited assets, state-linked mining, public investment and central-bank reserves are distinct statuses. They cannot be added into an unverified holding or treated as necessary steps toward a Bitcoin standard. Read [supply and control](/bitcoin/09-supply-and-control-who-holds-bitcoin-and-who-benefits/) and [country experiments](/bitcoin/04-countries-that-adopted-bitcoin-and-what-happened/).

### Paragraph 8

> Financial claims around Bitcoin can carry familiar risks. Custodians may fail to return assets, leverage may force sales, and a redeemable claim may fail if the issuer lacks sufficient accessible assets or cannot meet its contractual obligations; unclear backing or holder rights make that risk harder to assess. These are intermediary or contract risks, not a balance sheet of Bitcoin's base ledger. A stablecoin is designed to target a specified value; the label alone establishes neither cash/Treasury backing, every holder's redemption right, deposit insurance nor solvency. The [PCAOB investor-advocate staff advisory](https://pcaobus.org/resources/information-for-investors/investor-advisories/investor-advisory-exercise-caution-with-third-party-verification-proof-of-reserve-reports) warns that proof-of-reserve reports may verify selected assets at one time yet omit liabilities, holder rights or temporary borrowing; their scope depends on the stated procedures. Historical crisis comparisons identify possible run and contagion channels, not identical national crises or a forecast of their scale. Read [old crises in a new system](/bitcoin/12-how-bitcoin-could-reproduce-old-crises/) and [the limits of a global currency](/bitcoin/11-defects-that-stop-bitcoin-from-being-a-global-currency/).

### Paragraph 9

> The evidence in this short reading does not establish one winner for every monetary use. Physical gold remains an official reserve asset; that role does not establish gold-weight denomination for ordinary wages, prices and debts. State-issued money and bank claims can support credit or crisis liquidity, with results dependent on institutions, balance sheets and law. Bitcoin permits independent validation and self-custodied transfer, but these do not guarantee purchasing-power stability, unrestricted inclusion or a workable national unit of account. A Bitcoin-denominated economy remains a model requiring specified credit, legal, tax and crisis rules. Comparison should define a use—payment delivery, long-horizon purchasing power, contract pricing, credit, reserve liquidity or control—and use matched evidence. The final Bitcoin chapter offers revision criteria, not a universal verdict. Read [the final question](/bitcoin/13-is-bitcoin-the-answer/) and [the monetary regimes](/bitcoin/07-the-supply-system-fixed-rate-variable-rate-and-backing/).

### Paragraph 10

> Go deeper through a [reading path](/#/paths) for your question or the [research index](/#/research), then follow direct chapter source links where a claim matters. A chapter destination is a research path, not certification of all its assertions. Dated figures need a period and measure; current laws need enacted text, effective date and implementation. Broad causal claims need evidence matched to the event and population; this short reading cannot establish that evidence. Read across volumes with four distinctions: asset versus unit of account, base rules versus financial claims, adoption versus legal architecture, and historical explanation versus forecast.
